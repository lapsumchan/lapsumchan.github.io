About Me

My research connects genetics with statistical methodology for biomedical data. I was born and raised in Hong Kong and began my academic training in biomedical sciences, graduating with first-class honors before moving into biostatistics. Genetics has been a consistent thread across my work: I am interested in how genetic information can be combined with other complex biomedical data to study disease mechanisms, causal relationships, and individual risk. Over time, my methodological interests have expanded from high-dimensional genetic association and multi-omics problems to causal inference, survival analysis, and calibrated clinical risk prediction.

I am currently a Postdoctoral Fellow in Biostatistics at the Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health, working with Nilanjan Chatterjee. My current work focuses on calibrated risk prediction and survival modeling, with particular interest in translating genetic and longitudinal clinical information into reliable absolute-risk estimates and decision-relevant predictions.

Before joining Johns Hopkins, I was a postdoctoral researcher at the University of Minnesota, working with Wei Pan. My methodological work there included multivariable Mendelian randomization for highly correlated metabolite exposures, which commonly arise in metabolomics data, alongside collaborative cardiovascular risk-prediction research in the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities (ARIC) study.

I received my Ph.D. in Biostatistics from the University of Michigan, where I was advised by Peter X.K. Song and Gen Li. My doctoral research developed statistical methods for pleiotropy and multi-trait GWAS, censoring-aware microbiome differential abundance analysis, and diagnostics for instrumental-variable and Mendelian-randomization analyses.
