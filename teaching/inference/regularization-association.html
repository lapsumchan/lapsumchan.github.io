Chapter 10. Regularization, bias correction, and association inference

The normal-means calculation showed why accepting some bias can reduce total squared error. We now return to the conditional regression model, where many correlated markers can make individual coefficients unstable. Regularization restricts or penalizes those coefficients. Our immediate question is how to estimate a specified slope and assess its uncertainty when the penalized fit itself is biased. Good prediction of the response and a justified claim about one slope require different arguments.

Conditional regression defined the slope as a change in the conditional response mean when the other predictors are held fixed. We retain that target. An orthogonal design first makes the penalty bias visible. We then examine what additional structure permits estimation in high dimension, derive a residual-score correction, and assess the conditions needed for its standard errors and tests.

A penalty changes the estimation problem

For the slope calculations, we use the linear model y=Xβ+εy=X\beta+\varepsilon with pp slopes and no intercept in the following formulas. Here XX is n×pn\times p, while yy, β\beta, and ε\varepsilon are columns of lengths nn, pp, and nn, respectively. In this unit, pp is a predictor count rather than the event probability from the opening chapters. An unpenalized intercept belongs to a separate scalar, not to the pp-dimensional slope vector. We show exactly how centering removes that intercept in the worked example. Predictor scaling matters because penalties attach to numerical coefficients; rescaling one column changes how strongly its associated effect is penalized unless the fitting convention adjusts for that scale.

Ridge and lasso solve, respectively,

β̂ridge=argminb{∥y−Xb∥22n+λ2∥b∥22},β̂lasso=argminb{∥y−Xb∥22n+λ∥b∥1},λ≥0. \widehat\beta^{\mathrm{ridge}} =\arg\min_b\left\{\frac{\|y-Xb\|^2}{2n} +\frac{\lambda}{2}\|b\|_2^2\right\}, \qquad \widehat\beta^{\mathrm{lasso}} =\arg\min_b\left\{\frac{\|y-Xb\|^2}{2n} +\lambda\|b\|_1\right\},\quad \lambda\ge0.

The factors of two are part of our convention. Software using a different objective scaling can require a different numerical tuning parameter for the same solution. For λ>0\lambda>0, ridge has the unique solution

β̂ridge=(X𝖳Xn+λIp)−1X𝖳yn. \widehat\beta^{\mathrm{ridge}} =\left(\frac{X^\mathsf{T}X}{n}+\lambda I_p\right)^{-1} \frac{X^\mathsf{T}y}{n}.

It shrinks coefficients continuously. The lasso penalty is not differentiable at zero. Its optimality condition uses a subgradient sjs_j, equal to sign(β̂j)\operatorname{sign}(\widehat\beta_j) at a nonzero coefficient and lying in [−1,1][-1,1] at zero:

X𝖳(y−Xβ̂)n=λs. \frac{X^\mathsf{T}(y-X\widehat\beta)}{n}=\lambda s.

This condition explains how a coefficient can become exactly zero. The lasso objective is convex, but its coefficient minimizer need not be unique with an arbitrary rank-deficient design. A reported zero means the penalized rule selected zero at that tuning parameter; it is not a test accepting a scientific null.

Orthogonal geometry exposes shrinkage and its bias

When X𝖳X/n=IpX^\mathsf{T}X/n=I_p, put z=X𝖳y/nz=X^\mathsf{T}y/n. The squared-error part of the objective is, apart from a constant, 12∑j(bj−zj)2\frac12\sum_j(b_j-z_j)^2. Ridge and lasso therefore act coordinate by coordinate:

β̂jridge=zj1+λ,β̂jlasso=sign(zj)(|zj|−λ)+. \widehat\beta_j^{\mathrm{ridge}}=\frac{z_j}{1+\lambda}, \qquad \widehat\beta_j^{\mathrm{lasso}} =\operatorname{sign}(z_j)(|z_j|-\lambda)_+.

For the lasso, a positive minimizer solves b−zj+λ=0b-z_j+\lambda=0; a negative one solves b−zj−λ=0b-z_j-\lambda=0. When |zj|≤λ|z_j|\le\lambda, zero satisfies the subgradient condition. This derives soft thresholding rather than treating sparsity as a software property.

Under independent mean-zero errors with variance σ2\sigma^2, the orthogonal zjz_j has mean βj\beta_j and variance σ2/n\sigma^2/n. Ridge has squared-error risk

E(β̂jridge−βj)2=λ2(βj)2+σ2/n(1+λ)2. E(\widehat\beta_j^{\mathrm{ridge}}-\beta_j)^2 =\frac{\lambda^2(\beta_j)^2+\sigma^2/n}{(1+\lambda)^2}.

The variance decreases, but bias grows with signal size. Lasso adds a zero region and a nonregular sampling distribution. These are consequences of the chosen rule. As in the shrinkage chapter, improving one squared-error criterion does not establish improvement for each coefficient. Here the penalty acts on a regression design, so we need to examine that design before turning the fitted slopes into inferential claims.

High dimension requires structure, not just an optimizer

If p>np>n, the design has a nontrivial null space. For any vv with Xv=0Xv=0, the vectors β\beta and β+v\beta+v give the same conditional response mean. All unrestricted coefficients cannot then be identified from this fixed design. At p=np=n, an uncentered, full-rank square design without an intercept can identify the mean coefficients, but an ordinary residual variance estimate has no remaining degrees of freedom. Our centered design has rank at most n−1n-1, so that square full-rank case does not apply here.

Sparsity is one possible structural assumption: only ss coefficients are nonzero, or the vector is adequately approximated by a sparse one. Restricted design conditions require that relevant sparse changes in coefficients still produce detectable changes in XbXb. Nearly duplicate columns can defeat identification of individual effects even if they predict the same response well. Sparsity alone, without a compatible design and enough information relative to ss, is insufficient.

Choosing λ\lambda by validation targets the specified predictive loss on the validation population. This can be a sound prediction strategy. It does not prove a bound on coefficient bias, validate an inverse covariance estimate, or account for selection in a confidence interval. The inferential problem has to state its own conditions.

The bias correction and its remainder

For a population predictor vector on the working scale, write its positive-definite covariance as ΣX\Sigma_X and its precision as ΩX=ΣX−1\Omega_X=\Sigma_X^{-1}. The observed design supplies a different object:

ĈX=X𝖳Xn. \widehat C_X=\frac{X^\mathsf{T}X}{n}.

For centered predictors, R’s cov(X) divides by n−1n-1, so it equals nĈX/(n−1)n\widehat C_X/(n-1). The distinction matters when a matrix estimated with one divisor is used in a correction defined with another. When p>np>n, ĈX\widehat C_X is singular even if ΣX\Sigma_X is positive definite. We therefore estimate a useful approximate inverse from the design rather than attempting to invert the empirical Gram matrix.

Let Ω̂X\widehat\Omega_X be that design-based estimate, with size p×pp\times p, and let β̂\widehat\beta be an initial sparse slope estimate. We correct it using the residual score:

β̂db=β̂+1nΩ̂XX𝖳(y−Xβ̂). \widehat\beta_{\mathrm{db}} =\widehat\beta+ \frac{1}{n}\widehat\Omega_X X^\mathsf{T}(y-X\widehat\beta).

The lasso optimality condition tells us that the score has not generally vanished. Multiplying by an approximate inverse translates this remaining score into a coefficient adjustment. To see what the adjustment can and cannot remove, substitute y=Xβ+εy=X\beta+\varepsilon and collect terms:

β̂db−β=Ω̂XX𝖳εn+(Ip−Ω̂XĈX)(β̂−β)⏟r. \widehat\beta_{\mathrm{db}}-\beta =\widehat\Omega_X\frac{X^\mathsf{T}\varepsilon}{n} +\underbrace{(I_p-\widehat\Omega_X\widehat C_X) (\widehat\beta-\beta)}_{r}.

This identity holds exactly. The remainder rr records the initial coefficient error left after the correction. For coordinate jj,

|rj|≤∥ej𝖳−Ω̂X,jĈX∥∞∥β̂−β∥1, |r_j|\le \|e_j^\mathsf{T}-\widehat\Omega_{X,j}\widehat C_X\|_\infty \|\widehat\beta-\beta\|_1,

where eje_j is the jjth coordinate column and Ω̂X,j\widehat\Omega_{X,j} is row jj. The bound separates two tasks: control the sparse estimator’s error and approximate the inverse row well enough. Small prediction error alone does not control their product.

Suppose that, conditional on the design, the original errors are independent Gaussian with common variance σ2\sigma^2, and Ω̂X\widehat\Omega_X depends on the design alone. The leading term is then exactly conditionally Gaussian, with covariance

σ2nΩ̂XĈXΩ̂X𝖳. \frac{\sigma^2}{n} \widehat\Omega_X\widehat C_X\widehat\Omega_X^\mathsf{T}.

If we first center a model with an intercept, the centered errors are correlated; we will account for that projection explicitly below. Because the centered design is orthogonal to the constant column, the covariance of this leading slope term remains the expression above. This calculation describes the leading term, not the complete corrected estimator. A material remainder cannot be repaired by assigning a Gaussian reference distribution.

Estimate precision through nodewise regressions

The remainder bound asks for rows that approximately invert the predictor Gram matrix. We construct them one at a time. For each predictor column XjX_j, regress it on the remaining columns X−jX_{-j} using the same working scale:

γ̂j=argming{∥Xj−X−jg∥22n+λj∥g∥1},τ̂j2=∥Xj−X−jγ̂j∥2n+λj∥γ̂j∥1. \widehat\gamma_j= \arg\min_g\left\{\frac{\|X_j-X_{-j}g\|^2}{2n} +\lambda_j\|g\|_1\right\}, \qquad \widehat\tau_j^2= \frac{\|X_j-X_{-j}\widehat\gamma_j\|^2}{n} +\lambda_j\|\widehat\gamma_j\|_1.

Here γ̂j\widehat\gamma_j has p−1p-1 entries. Let cjc_j be the row with a one in position jj and −γ̂j-\widehat\gamma_j in the other positions. Set Ω̂X,j=cj/τ̂j2\widehat\Omega_{X,j}=c_j/\widehat\tau_j^2. The penalty contribution in τ̂j2\widehat\tau_j^2 is essential: the nodewise lasso optimality condition gives

(Ω̂XĈX)jj=1,maxk≠j|(Ω̂XĈX)jk|≤λjτ̂j2 (\widehat\Omega_X\widehat C_X)_{jj}=1, \qquad \max_{k\ne j}|(\widehat\Omega_X\widehat C_X)_{jk}| \le\frac{\lambda_j}{\widehat\tau_j^2}

for an exact optimizer. To obtain the diagonal identity, multiply the nodewise residual by Xj=X−jγ̂j+residualX_j=X_{-j}\widehat\gamma_j+\text{residual}; the cross-product with the fitted part contributes nλj∥γ̂j∥1n\lambda_j\|\widehat\gamma_j\|_1. The off-diagonal bound is the remaining subgradient bound. These relations explain why the residual variance alone would give the wrong normalization. [5]

The resulting estimate need not be symmetric, and it is not the ordinary inverse of ĈX\widehat C_X. We retain its row orientation in the correction and use its transpose in the covariance calculation. Its statistical accuracy still requires a suitable sparse or approximately sparse population precision, well-behaved predictor covariance, and appropriate tuning. The optimality identities establish algebraic control, not every condition required for inference.

Conditions for coefficient intervals and p-values

For one prespecified coordinate, write vj=(Ω̂XĈXΩ̂X𝖳)jjv_j=(\widehat\Omega_X\widehat C_X\widehat\Omega_X^\mathsf{T})_{jj}. A sufficient asymptotic argument requires a correctly specified linear conditional mean, independent homoscedastic Gaussian errors before centering, a design-based Ω̂X\widehat\Omega_X, nondegenerate vjv_j, a consistent estimate σ̂/σ→P1\widehat\sigma/\sigma\xrightarrow{P}1, and

nrjσvj→P0. \frac{\sqrt n\,r_j}{\sigma\sqrt{v_j}}\xrightarrow{P}0.

The leading Gaussian term and this negligible-remainder condition give an asymptotic standard normal pivot. We estimate its covariance by

V̂db=σ̂2nΩ̂XĈXΩ̂X𝖳,sêj=(V̂db)jj. \widehat V_{\mathrm{db}}= \frac{\widehat\sigma^2}{n} \widehat\Omega_X\widehat C_X\widehat\Omega_X^\mathsf{T}, \qquad \widehat{\mathrm{se}}_j= \sqrt{(\widehat V_{\mathrm{db}})_{jj}}.

The descriptive subscript separates this coefficient covariance from the false-discovery count VV in multiplicity and the local risk-set covariance in the survival book. For H0j:βj=0H_{0j}:\beta_j=0, the interval, statistic, and two-sided p-value are

β̂db,j±z1−α/2sêj,Zj=β̂db,jsêj,Pj=2Φ(−|Zj|), \widehat\beta_{\mathrm{db},j} \pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\widehat{\mathrm{se}}_j, \qquad Z_j=\frac{\widehat\beta_{\mathrm{db},j}} {\widehat{\mathrm{se}}_j}, \qquad P_j=2\Phi(-|Z_j|),

where Φ\Phi is the standard normal CDF and z1−α/2z_{1-\alpha/2} its quantile. These are asymptotic constructions. A zero lasso coefficient can have a nonzero corrected estimate; we calculate the p-value for each specified coordinate, including those that the lasso set to zero.

One common sufficient rate calculation combines an initial ℓ1\ell_1 error of order slogp/ns\sqrt{\log p/n} with an inverse-row approximation error of order logp/n\sqrt{\log p/n}. If the variance factors stay bounded away from zero, the resulting remainder is negligible on the n−1/2n^{-1/2} scale when slogp/n→0s\log p/\sqrt n\to0. We need sparsity, restricted design conditions, suitable penalty levels, and accurate inverse estimation to obtain these rates. A fitted model’s predictive performance does not establish them.

For a growing collection of tests, coordinatewise convergence alone does not justify a familywise or false-discovery guarantee. The approximation must be accurate enough over the tested family, and the p-value dependence must support the adjustment. Multiplicity explains why Holm permits arbitrary dependence between valid null p-values, while BH requires additional dependence conditions. Neither adjustment can repair invalid input p-values.

A complete correlated, high-dimensional example

I use a new synthetic continuous response so that we can compare every estimate with a known coefficient. This is a separate dataset from the earlier low-dimensional regression and normal-means examples. We generate n=240n=240 observations and p=360p=360 predictors with population covariance (ΣX,raw)jk=0.4|j−k|(\Sigma_{X,\mathrm{raw}})_{jk}=0.4^{|j-k|}. The original model is

yraw,i=2+xraw,i𝖳βraw+εraw,i,εraw,i∼iidN(0,1), y_{\mathrm{raw},i}=2+x_{\mathrm{raw},i}^\mathsf{T}\beta_{\mathrm{raw}} +\varepsilon_{\mathrm{raw},i}, \qquad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{raw},i}\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}N(0,1),

independently of the predictors. The only nonzero slopes are βraw,1=0.7\beta_{\mathrm{raw},1}=0.7, βraw,5=−0.55\beta_{\mathrm{raw},5}=-0.55, and βraw,12=0.4\beta_{\mathrm{raw},12}=0.4. These are conditional associations in the specified model, not causal effects inferred from the simulation.

Let M=In−𝟏n𝟏n𝖳/nM=I_n-\mathbf1_n\mathbf1_n^\mathsf{T}/n and let DD be the diagonal matrix of empirical predictor RMS scales after centering. The working data are

X=MXrawD−1,y=Myraw,β=Dβraw. X=MX_{\mathrm{raw}}D^{-1},\qquad y=My_{\mathrm{raw}},\qquad \beta=D\beta_{\mathrm{raw}}.

Thus X𝖳𝟏n=0X^\mathsf{T}\mathbf1_n=0, each diagonal of ĈX\widehat C_X is one, and the working coefficient measures response change per empirical RMS unit of the predictor. We do not standardize the response. Under a fixed diagonal change of scale, population covariance and precision transform as D−1ΣX,rawD−1D^{-1}\Sigma_{X,\mathrm{raw}}D^{-1} and DΩX,rawDD\Omega_{X,\mathrm{raw}}D, respectively. The fitted precision must use the actual scale of XX in the correction; estimating it on the raw columns and inserting it unchanged would be inconsistent.

The working error is Mεraw∼Nn(0,σ2M)M\varepsilon_{\mathrm{raw}}\sim N_n(0,\sigma^2M), not an independent-error vector. Nevertheless, X𝖳M=X𝖳X^\mathsf{T}M=X^\mathsf{T}, so the leading-term covariance remains σ2Ω̂XĈXΩ̂X𝖳/n\sigma^2\widehat\Omega_X\widehat C_X\widehat\Omega_X^\mathsf{T}/n. Conditioning on the realized design and its scales makes the working truth DβrawD\beta_{\mathrm{raw}} a specified comparison target. Dividing the fitted slopes by those same scales returns the original-unit coefficients. We then recover the unpenalized intercept as y¯raw−x¯raw𝖳β̂db,raw\overline y_{\mathrm{raw}}-\overline x_{\mathrm{raw}}^\mathsf{T}\widehat\beta_{\mathrm{db,raw}}; the example does not attach a confidence interval to this intercept.

From first principles: estimate the noise and the sparse slopes

When p>np>n, ordinary least-squares residual degrees of freedom do not provide a noise estimate. We use the scaled lasso [7], which jointly minimizes

∥y−Xb∥22nu+u2+λ0∥b∥1,u>0. \frac{\|y-Xb\|^2}{2nu}+\frac{u}{2} +\lambda_0\|b\|_1,\qquad u>0.

Here uu is a candidate noise SD, distinct from the sparsity count ss used above. For a fixed uu, minimizing over bb is a lasso fit with penalty λ=uλ0\lambda=u\lambda_0. For a fixed bb, differentiation with respect to uu gives u=∥y−Xb∥/nu=\|y-Xb\|/\sqrt n. Alternating these two steps yields β̂\widehat\beta and σ̂\widehat\sigma. The transparent coordinate-descent implementation below uses the same soft thresholding derived earlier.

We set λ0=1.12log(p)/n\lambda_0=1.1\sqrt{2\log(p)/n}, and use this same explicit numerical level in every nodewise regression. The level has the usual log(p)/n\sqrt{\log(p)/n} order; the constant is a numerical choice for this example, not a claim of uniform coverage. Scaled-lasso noise consistency requires the sparse linear model and suitable compatibility and tuning conditions. Dividing by nn is part of that estimator, not an exact unbiased residual-variance formula. Centering loses one direction, but its effect on this asymptotic scale estimate vanishes as nn grows. We neither subtract pp from nn nor treat the selected model size as an exact residual degree-of-freedom correction.

Standard R workflow and the correction

We use glmnet::glmnet() for the nodewise fits and for an independent implementation of the final lasso objective. We explicitly set the penalty sequence, alpha = 1, intercept = FALSE, and standardize = FALSE. The design has already been centered and scaled. The package reports coefficients on the supplied predictor scale; supplying the exact penalty and comparing the two optimizers avoids relying on its automatically generated penalty path. We perform the correction and standard-error calculation directly from the displayed matrix formulas. [8]

Dependencies: base R, stats, and glmnet; installing glmnet also installs its package dependencies. The block contains every helper, the data generator, both fitting routes, and the inference calculations. The nodewise helper adapts the row construction in the published DrFARM repository’s precM.NL.one() and precM.NL.whole(), with explicit tuning and no internal rescaling. It uses only predictor regressions. [6] The adapted code notice and GNU GPL version 3 license accompany the example.

options(width = 110)

# From first principles: minimize RSS/(2n) + lambda * sum(abs(beta)).
# Updating one coefficient reduces to soft thresholding its partial residual.
soft.threshold <- function(z, lambda) sign(z) * pmax(abs(z) - lambda, 0)
lasso.cd <- function(X, y, lambda, beta.start = rep(0, ncol(X)),
                     tolerance = 1e-10, max.sweeps = 10000L) {
  n <- nrow(X)
  C.hat.X <- crossprod(X) / n
  score <- drop(crossprod(X, y) / n)
  beta.hat <- beta.start
  gradient <- score - drop(C.hat.X %*% beta.hat)
  for (sweep in seq_len(max.sweeps)) {
    largest.change <- 0
    for (j in seq_len(ncol(X))) {
      old <- beta.hat[j]
      beta.hat[j] <- soft.threshold(gradient[j] + C.hat.X[j, j] * old,
                                     lambda) / C.hat.X[j, j]
      change <- beta.hat[j] - old
      gradient <- gradient - C.hat.X[, j] * change
      largest.change <- max(largest.change, abs(change))
    }
    if (largest.change < tolerance) return(beta.hat)
  }
  stop("Coordinate descent did not converge.")
}

# Scaled lasso alternates a lasso fit and the residual RMS noise estimate.
# This solves RSS/(2 n sigma) + sigma/2 + lambda.base * ||beta||_1.
scaled.lasso <- function(X, y, lambda.base, tolerance = 1e-9,
                         max.steps = 1000L) {
  beta.hat <- rep(0, ncol(X))
  sigma.hat <- sqrt(mean(y^2))
  for (step in seq_len(max.steps)) {
    beta.hat <- lasso.cd(X, y, lambda.base * sigma.hat, beta.hat)
    sigma.new <- sqrt(mean((y - drop(X %*% beta.hat))^2))
    if (sigma.new <= 0) stop("Scaled lasso reached a zero residual scale.")
    if (abs(sigma.new - sigma.hat) <= tolerance * sigma.hat) {
      return(list(beta.hat = beta.hat, sigma.hat = sigma.new,
                  lambda = lambda.base * sigma.new, steps = step))
    }
    sigma.hat <- sigma.new
  }
  stop("Scaled lasso did not converge.")
}

# Minimal nodewise precision calculation, with the original row orientation.
# The penalty contribution in tau.sq makes each diagonal inverse product one.
nodewise.precision <- function(X, lambda.node) {
  n <- nrow(X)
  p <- ncol(X)
  Omega.hat.X <- matrix(0, p, p)
  nodewise.kkt <- numeric(p)
  for (j in seq_len(p)) {
    other <- setdiff(seq_len(p), j)
    # The requested decreasing path ends exactly at the chosen penalty.
    lambda.max <- max(abs(drop(crossprod(X[, other, drop = FALSE], X[, j]) / n)))
    lambda.path <- sort(unique(c(max(lambda.max, lambda.node) * 1.01,
                                 lambda.node)), decreasing = TRUE)
    fit <- glmnet::glmnet(X[, other, drop = FALSE], X[, j],
                          family = "gaussian", alpha = 1,
                          lambda = lambda.path, intercept = FALSE,
                          standardize = FALSE, thresh = 1e-12, maxit = 100000L)
    gamma.hat <- drop(as.matrix(fit$beta)[, ncol(fit$beta)])
    residual <- X[, j] - drop(X[, other, drop = FALSE] %*% gamma.hat)
    score <- drop(crossprod(X[, other, drop = FALSE], residual) / n)
    active <- abs(gamma.hat) > 1e-10
    nodewise.kkt[j] <- max(c(abs(score[active] - lambda.node *
                                 sign(gamma.hat[active])),
                             pmax(abs(score[!active]) - lambda.node, 0)))
    tau.sq <- mean(residual^2) + lambda.node * sum(abs(gamma.hat))
    Omega.hat.X[j, j] <- 1 / tau.sq
    Omega.hat.X[j, other] <- -gamma.hat / tau.sq
  }
  attr(Omega.hat.X, "max.nodewise.kkt") <- max(nodewise.kkt)
  Omega.hat.X
}

se.db <- sqrt(diag(V.hat.db))

P <- 2 * stats::pnorm(-abs(Z))

Execute the geometry and audit a failure

The next block contains three separate experiments, each with newly generated data. The first uses three orthogonal columns to compare ridge, lasso, and a residual correction. The second constructs an intentionally high-dimensional setting where the expansion is exact but its remainder is large. The third selects a marker in one sample and estimates its slope in an independent sample. Dependencies: base R and stats, included with R. No external files are required.

# Regularization and association: exact geometry and an honest split-sample fit.
# Dependencies: base R and stats (included in R).
options(width = 95)

# From first principles: a centered orthogonal design with X'X/n = I.
X <- cbind(x1 = c(1, 1, 1, 1, -1, -1, -1, -1),
           x2 = c(1, 1, -1, -1, 1, 1, -1, -1),
           x3 = c(1, -1, 1, -1, 1, -1, 1, -1))

C.hat.X <- crossprod(X) / n
z <- drop(crossprod(X, y) / n)
lambda <- .4
ridge <- drop(solve(C.hat.X + lambda * diag(ncol(X)), z))
soft.threshold <- function(z, lambda) sign(z) * pmax(abs(z) - lambda, 0)

                 lasso = lasso, corrected = debiased),
      digits = 6, row.names = FALSE)

            max(abs(debiased - z))))

The first table derives the lasso zeros and ridge shrinkage directly. In the orthogonal setting, taking Ω̂X=Ip\widehat\Omega_X=I_p gives β̂db=z\widehat\beta_{\mathrm{db}}=z exactly, so the correction removes the lasso bias in this special geometry. Under the Gaussian model it would recover ordinary least-squares inference. This calculation is a check of an identity, not an illustration of a general high-dimensional theorem.

In the 12-person, 18-predictor example, we center the generated predictor columns and the error vector. The expansion discrepancy is about 2.2×10−162.2\times10^{-16} while the largest remainder is about 11.65 conditional noise standard deviations. The initial estimator and approximate inverse here are ridge constructions chosen for an algebra audit. No high-dimensional p-value is reported. Perfect agreement with the expansion can coexist with an unusable normal approximation: checking code algebra and establishing inferential validity are different tasks.

Independent selection clarifies a different valid argument

The correction approach needs a negligible bias remainder. The last experiment uses a different route to a valid low-dimensional argument: marker selection uses only the discovery sample. Conditional on that selection, the independent inference sample treats the chosen marker as prespecified. For a correctly specified low-dimensional Gaussian linear model with full rank and positive residual degrees of freedom, its ordinary tt interval has the conditional repeated-sampling guarantee derived in conditional regression. With known σ\sigma, a corresponding normal interval is available; estimating σ\sigma by the residual mean square gives the exact tt construction under those Gaussian assumptions. [1]

An omitted active predictor can change the target or the error model. Here all predictor columns are independently generated normal variables. Conditional on the selected predictor, omitted signals are independent normal variation, so the selected-marker slope remains its full-model coefficient and the reduced conditional model is Gaussian. With correlated predictors, a reduced-model slope generally differs from the full-model conditional coefficient. Independence between samples prevents selection from using the inference noise; it does not eliminate omitted-variable bias or create a target that the fitted model does not identify.

The code selects marker 1 and estimates a slope about 0.720264, with a 95% interval about 0.473669 to 0.966858. The generator’s coefficient is 0.9. One interval covering that value does not prove coverage; the coverage argument comes from the model, independent selection, and the pivot. Splitting sacrifices sample size and can produce unstable selections, but it makes clear which data choose the question and which data quantify its uncertainty.
