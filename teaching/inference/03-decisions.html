Chapter 4. Can a better estimate lead to a worse decision?

An estimator can be better at estimating a probability and worse at choosing an action. To compare them, we need to say what “better” means. Squared error asks how far a reported probability is from the truth. A decision loss asks what happens when that report changes an action. The first comparison averages the size of numerical errors; the second may care almost entirely about errors near one threshold.

This chapter develops one small experiment far enough that every comparison can be checked without an asymptotic approximation. The same two estimates will be evaluated as parameter estimates, predictions for a new outcome, scores on reused training outcomes, and inputs to a binary policy. We will also change the averaging distribution, from a fixed true probability to a prior over probabilities. Following each change of expectation lets us see which comparisons survive and which require a new calculation.

The regression chapter separated coefficient estimates from fitted probabilities. We now return to the independent Bernoulli model from the first two chapters to compare two procedures under explicit losses. Keeping that model small lets us calculate the bias, variance and expected loss of each rule exactly; it does not replace the conditional targets introduced by regression. A fresh binary outcome and a decision with specified costs add two different uses of the estimate. The action is a classification choice; evaluating a treatment that changes the outcome would require a causal model as well as a loss function.

4.1 One experiment, several questions

Let X1,…,XnX_1,\ldots,X_n be independent Bernoulli variables with common probability pp, and let K=∑i=1nXiK=\sum_{i=1}^n X_i. Initially pp is fixed, although unknown to the analyst. Repeating the experiment generates a new training sample and hence a new K∼Binomial(n,p)K\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(n,p). Consider qU=Kn,qS=K+1n+2=nn+2qU+2n+212.q_U=\frac K n,\qquad q_S=\frac{K+1}{n+2} =\frac{n}{n+2}q_U+\frac{2}{n+2}\frac12. \label{i3-estimators}(4.1) The subscript UU denotes the unbiased sample proportion. The subscript SS denotes shrinkage toward one half. Both are functions of observable data; neither uses the unknown pp. Shrinkage is stronger for smaller samples and prevents reports exactly equal to zero or one.

We distinguish a reported probability qq from an action a∈{0,1}a\in\{0,1\}. A rule maps the observed sample to a report or an action; its risk is expected loss under a specified distribution. Thus an assertion of superiority must identify the target, loss, comparison class, and expectation. Here our main comparison class contains just the two rules in (4.1); we make no claim of optimality among every possible rule.

This is a homogeneous population model: there are no covariates and every training subject and future subject shares the same event probability. That restriction lets us isolate estimation from action. In a heterogeneous population, both prediction error and decision cost would additionally average over the covariate distribution. A claim about one common probability should not silently become a claim about calibration in subgroups or the value of individualized treatment.

4.2 Squared-error risk and the bias–variance tradeoff

Under squared-error loss, frequentist risk is Rp(q)=𝔼p[(q−p)2]=Varp(q)+{𝔼p(q)−p}2.R_p(q)=\mathbb{E}_p[(q-p)^2] =\operatorname{Var}_p(q)+\{\mathbb{E}_p(q)-p\}^2. \label{i3-mse-decomposition}(4.2) To see the second equality, write q−p=(q−𝔼pq)+(𝔼pq−p)q-p=(q-\mathbb{E}_p q)+(\mathbb{E}_p q-p), square, and take expectations. The cross term vanishes because 𝔼p(q−𝔼pq)=0\mathbb{E}_p(q-\mathbb{E}_p q)=0. This calculation assumes a finite second moment, which both bounded reports have.

Since 𝔼pK=np\mathbb{E}_pK=np and Varp(K)=np(1−p)\operatorname{Var}_p(K)=np(1-p), the sample proportion has zero bias and variance p(1−p)/np(1-p)/n. For the shrinkage rule, 𝔼p(qS)−p=1−2pn+2,Varp(qS)=np(1−p)(n+2)2.\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}_p(q_S)-p&=\frac{1-2p}{n+2},\label{i3-bias}\\ \operatorname{Var}_p(q_S)&=\frac{np(1-p)}{(n+2)^2}. \label{i3-variance} \end{aligned}(4.3, 4.4) Its bias is positive below one half, negative above one half, and zero at one half. Its variance is always smaller than that of qUq_U for 0<p<10<p<1. Smaller variance alone does not settle the MSE comparison, because the squared bias must be included: Rp(qU)=p(1−p)n,Rp(qS)=np(1−p)+(1−2p)2(n+2)2.R_p(q_U)=\frac{p(1-p)}n,\qquad R_p(q_S)=\frac{np(1-p)+(1-2p)^2}{(n+2)^2}. \label{i3-mse}(4.5)

At n=10,p=0.15n=10,p=0.15, these risks are 0.012750000.01275000 and 0.012256940.01225694. The improvement is real but small. It is also local in the parameter space. Subtraction, using (1−2p)2=1−4p(1−p)(1-2p)^2=1-4p(1-p), gives Rp(qS)−Rp(qU)=n−4(2n+1)p(1−p)n(n+2)2.R_p(q_S)-R_p(q_U) =\frac{n-4(2n+1)p(1-p)}{n(n+2)^2}. \label{i3-mse-difference}(4.6) Shrinkage wins precisely when p(1−p)>n/{4(2n+1)}p(1-p)>n/\{4(2n+1)\}. At n=10n=10 the improvement interval is approximately (0.13813,0.86187)(0.13813,0.86187). At p=0p=0, by contrast, K=0K=0 certainly and qUq_U has zero error, whereas qS=1/(n+2)q_S=1/(n+2) has positive error. Therefore shrinkage does not dominate the sample proportion under squared error over the full parameter space.

There is no conflict with a minimum-variance-unbiased theorem. Such a theorem compares unbiased estimators; qSq_S is generally outside that class. Nor does the existence of a complete sufficient count make every function of the count optimal for every loss. The qualification “of its expected value” in the Lehmann–Scheffé formulation matters.

4.3 Prediction for an independent outcome

Squared error has compared the reports with the unknown probability. We next compare the same reports with an outcome that has not yet occurred. Now introduce a fresh outcome Y∼Bernoulli(p)Y\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(p), independent of the training sample at fixed pp. Use q(K)q(K) to predict YY and score the prediction by the Brier loss (Y−q)2(Y-q)^2. Conditioning on the training count fixes the report, so 𝔼p[(Y−q)2∣K]=p(1−q)2+(1−p)q2=p(1−p)+(q−p)2.\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}_p[(Y-q)^2\mid K] &=p(1-q)^2+(1-p)q^2\\ &=p(1-p)+(q-p)^2. \label{i3-brier-conditional} \end{aligned}(4.7) A further expectation over KK yields Bp(q):=𝔼p[(Y−q(K))2]=p(1−p)+Rp(q).B_p(q):=\mathbb{E}_p[(Y-q(K))^2]=p(1-p)+R_p(q). \label{i3-brier-fresh}(4.8) The first term is outcome variation even if pp were known. The second is the cost of estimating it. Because the first term is identical for both rules, the MSE ordering transfers exactly to fresh-outcome Brier risk in this experiment. At the chosen pp, the Brier risks are 0.140250000.14025000 and 0.139756940.13975694.

Independence is doing substantive work. We conditioned on training data while leaving the outcome distribution Bernoulli(p)(p). This describes a new exchangeable individual under the model, not a subject selected according to their outcome and not a later population with a different event probability. The decomposition is a statement about that evaluation design; it does not establish a universal ranking under distribution change.

For a fixed report, (4.7) also explains strict propriety: the expected score is uniquely minimized at q=pq=p. But our reports fluctuate with the training sample. Propriety supplies a target, while repeated-sampling risk compares how two procedures approach that target. Neither statement says that converting each report into a discontinuous action preserves its ordering under a different loss. We need both statements: propriety identifies the probability target, while risk evaluates a procedure for estimating it.

4.4 Why reusing an outcome changes the answer

Suppose instead that the report is scored against the observations used to calculate it. Write U=K/nU=K/n and define the training-average score B̂train(q)=1n∑i=1n(Xi−q)2=U(1−U)+(q−U)2.\widehat{B}_{\mathrm{train}}(q)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n(X_i-q)^2 =U(1-U)+(q-U)^2. \label{i3-training-identity}(4.9) The identity follows by expanding the square, using Xi2=XiX_i^2=X_i, and collecting terms. For every realized sample, q=Uq=U minimizes this score over all constant reports. Consequently qUq_U always looks at least as good as qSq_S on the data it summarized. This is an optimization fact, not independent evidence of better prediction.

We can quantify the optimism rather than merely warn about it. For any report q(K)q(K), expansion of the fresh and training expectations gives Bp(q)−𝔼pB̂train(q)=2{𝔼p(qU)−p𝔼p(q)}=2Covp(q,U).B_p(q)-\mathbb{E}_p\widehat{B}_{\mathrm{train}}(q) =2\{\mathbb{E}_p(qU)-p\mathbb{E}_p(q)\} =2\operatorname{Cov}_p(q,U). \label{i3-optimism}(4.10) For these affine rules the covariance is positive: the observed success count increases both the fitted report and the outcomes used to judge it. Since Var(U)=p(1−p)/n\operatorname{Var}(U)=p(1-p)/n, the optimism is 2p(1−p)/n2p(1-p)/n for qUq_U and 2p(1−p)/(n+2)2p(1-p)/(n+2) for qSq_S.

The expected reused scores are therefore 0.114750000.11475000 and 0.118506940.11850694, respectively. The reused score favors qUq_U, although the fresh score favors qSq_S. Shrinkage reduces the degree of optimism as well as changing the report. Reporting only the smaller training score would conceal both facts. Scoring one fixed training observation has the same expectation here by exchangeability; it remains dependent evaluation.

4.5 A threshold policy and its actual cost

Let action 1 mean making a positive decision. A false positive costs 3 and a false negative costs 10, with zero cost for either correct decision: L(a,Y)=3a(1−Y)+10(1−a)Y.L(a,Y)=3a(1-Y)+10(1-a)Y. \label{i3-action-loss}(4.11) These are illustrative units, not clinical utilities. If a probability qq is used in the calculation, the expected costs of actions 1 and 0 are 3(1−q)3(1-q) and 10q10q. Action 1 is strictly preferred when 3(1−q)<10q⇔q>313.3(1-q)<10q\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad q>\frac3{13}. \label{i3-threshold}(4.12) We adopt action 0 at equality. Either action minimizes the calculated cost at an exact tie, but fixing the convention makes a policy reproducible.

For n=10n=10, qU>3/13q_U>3/13 is equivalent to K≥3K\ge3, whereas qS>3/13q_S>3/13 is equivalent to K≥2K\ge2. Their actions differ only at K=2K=2: the reports are 0.200.20 and 0.250.25, on opposite sides of 3/13≈0.230773/13\approx0.23077. Neither report ever equals the threshold in this sample size. 4.6 A Bayes rule is not a pointwise guarantee

The same shrinkage report has a Bayesian interpretation. Put a fixed Beta(1,1)\operatorname{Beta}(1,1) prior on pp. Multiplying its density by the binomial likelihood gives p∣K∼Beta(K+1,n−K+1)p\mid K\sim\operatorname{Beta}(K+1,n-K+1), whose mean is qSq_S. For a proposed report qq, posterior squared-error loss decomposes as 𝔼[(p−q)2∣K]=Var(p∣K)+{𝔼[p∣K]−q}2.\mathbb{E}[(p-q)^2\mid K]=\operatorname{Var}(p\mid K) +\{\mathbb{E}[p\mid K]-q\}^2. \label{i3-posterior-square}(4.15) Thus the posterior mean minimizes posterior expected squared error. It is not the definition of a Bayes action for every loss; an absolute-error loss instead leads to a posterior median. Posterior expected loss is the general principle.

Under the specified prior-predictive model, ℙ(Y=1∣K)=𝔼[p∣K]=qS\mathbb{P}(Y=1\mid K)=\mathbb{E}[p\mid K]=q_S. Thresholding qSq_S consequently minimizes posterior expected loss (4.11) too. This conclusion and the fixed-pp cost increase are simultaneously correct. One expectation averages over the stated posterior; the other evaluates training samples and new outcomes at p=0.15p=0.15.

Integrating (4.5) over the uniform prior gives 1/(6n)1/(6n) for qUq_U and 1/{6(n+2)}1/\{6(n+2)\} for qSq_S. The prior-averaged decision costs at n=10n=10 are 14/1114/11 and 5/45/4, favoring the shrinkage policy by 1/441/44. For the decision cost, the prior-predictive count distribution is uniform on 0,…,100,\ldots,10. The policies differ only at K=2K=2, where the posterior predictive event probability is 1/41/4. Choosing action 1 then costs 3(3/4)=9/43(3/4)=9/4, rather than 10(1/4)=5/210(1/4)=5/2 for action 0. The shrinkage policy saves 1/41/4 in that state; weighting it by the state probability 1/111/11 gives the saving 1/441/44. A Bayes improvement is therefore a prior-weighted improvement, not a claim at every parameter value. Calling the uniform prior “uninformative” does not remove its effect near a decision boundary. Estimating prior parameters or introducing a hierarchy changes the procedure again. The fixed-prior calculation here does not justify silently treating a prior fitted from these outcomes as if it had been specified beforehand. 4.7 Exact calculations and paired simulation

The complete R example evaluates all eleven count states, checks the analytic equalities numerically, and produces the figures and tables. It also simulates 200,000 independent training-count/new-outcome pairs with seed 20261004. Both procedures use the same pair in each replication. For paired loss differences Db=LS,b−LU,bD_b=L_{S,b}-L_{U,b}, the Monte Carlo standard error is MCSÊ(D¯)=sDB.\widehat{\operatorname{MCSE}}(\overline D) =\frac{s_D}{\sqrt B}. \label{i3-mcse}(4.16) This quantifies simulation error in the estimated expectation, not uncertainty about a fitted probability for a particular dataset. Pairing retains the covariance between procedures. Adding separate marginal variance estimates would discard that information.

One can instead integrate out the new outcome and simulate only the conditional cost given KK. That estimator has the same expectation but a smaller Monte Carlo variance; its error bar must be calculated from its own replication values. Similarly, many validation subjects evaluated with one training sample are not independent training replications. A simulation should repeat the entire experiment when its target averages over refitting, or clearly state that it conditions on the fitted rule. Here exact enumeration makes such distinctions visible and avoids choosing a simulation size merely to obtain a desired result.
