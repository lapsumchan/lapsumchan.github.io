Chapter 3. Regression and conditional targets

A biomarker study asks whether a marker is associated with an outcome after accounting for age. A prediction study asks what outcome to expect for a person with a particular age and marker value. Both questions can use regression, but they do not have the same target. The coefficient is a property of a specified conditional model or approximation; the prediction is a function of the available information. Neither is automatically the effect of intervening on the marker.

The probability models and likelihood calculations developed earlier extend naturally to this setting. Instead of one common outcome distribution, we specify how the outcome distribution depends on observed predictors. This section develops linear and logistic regression together so that the choice of outcome, target, fitting objective, and interpretation remains visible. The small worked dataset is synthetic and has no clinical operating meaning.

Information and the coefficient target

Let Z=(Z1,…,Zp)𝖳Z=(Z_1,\ldots,Z_p)^\mathsf{T} be a p×1p\times1 predictor column measured before the outcome. Here pp counts slopes and excludes the intercept. We add the intercept through Z+=(1,Z𝖳)𝖳Z_+=(1,Z^\mathsf{T})^\mathsf{T} and β+=(β0,β𝖳)𝖳\beta_+=(\beta_0,\beta^\mathsf{T})^\mathsf{T}, both of dimension (p+1)×1(p+1)\times1; β\beta contains the pp slopes. The coefficient and predictor columns have the same order. R stores the intercept in design column 1, followed by the pp predictor columns. For a continuous response VV, a useful starting target is the best linear approximation under squared loss:

β+†=argminb+E{(V−Z+𝖳b+)2}. \beta_+^\dagger=\arg\min_{b_+} E\{(V-Z_+^\mathsf{T}b_+)^2\}.

Assume the relevant second moments exist and E(Z+Z+𝖳)E(Z_+Z_+^\mathsf{T}) is invertible. Differentiating the expected loss gives the normal equations and their unique solution:

E{Z+(V−Z+𝖳β+†)}=0,β+†={E(Z+Z+𝖳)}−1E(Z+V). E\{Z_+(V-Z_+^\mathsf{T}\beta_+^\dagger)\}=0, \qquad \beta_+^\dagger=\{E(Z_+Z_+^\mathsf{T})\}^{-1}E(Z_+V).

Write β†\beta^\dagger for the last pp entries of β+†\beta_+^\dagger, the projected slopes. This defines a target even when E(V∣Z)E(V\mid Z) is nonlinear. If instead the conditional mean model E(V∣Z)=Z+𝖳β+E(V\mid Z)=Z_+^\mathsf{T}\beta_+ is correct, the approximation target equals its coefficient vector. A zero projection coefficient therefore has a narrower interpretation than conditional independence, and a linear approximation does not justify a normal error distribution.

The adjustment set matters. For a particular predictor ZjZ_j, j=1,…,pj=1,\ldots,p, subtract its population linear projection onto all the other columns, including the intercept, to obtain a residual RjR_j. Provided E(Rj2)>0E(R_j^2)>0, the corresponding coefficient satisfies

βj†=E(RjV)E(Rj2). \beta_j^\dagger=\frac{E(R_jV)}{E(R_j^2)}.

The coefficient describes outcome variation aligned with the part of ZjZ_j not linearly explained by the other included predictors. Changing those predictors changes RjR_j and can change the coefficient. Correlated genetic variants provide an important example: marginal association with one variant and association conditional on other variants are different scientific statements. A variable’s units matter too; an age coefficient per ten years is ten times the coefficient per year.

Linear estimation and uncertainty

The population equations define the coefficient target. To estimate it, we replace population averages by averages over the observed people. This yields least squares; to derive an uncertainty statement, we will then add a sampling model. For nn people, let X+X_+ be the n×(p+1)n\times(p+1) design matrix with rows z+,i𝖳z_{+,i}^\mathsf{T} and let vv be the response vector. Minimizing ∥v−X+b+∥2\|v-X_+b_+\|^2 gives

β̂+=(X+𝖳X+)−1X+𝖳v, \widehat\beta_+=(X_+^\mathsf{T}X_+)^{-1}X_+^\mathsf{T}v,

when X+X_+ has full column rank. Its first component estimates the intercept; its last pp components form the slope estimate β̂\widehat\beta. For numerical work, a QR decomposition is generally preferable to explicitly forming the inverse. We use the direct formula to connect the normal equations to the code, while retaining QR decomposition for a numerically stable routine analysis.

To quantify uncertainty, we now specify a conditional sampling model in addition to the minimization problem. Suppose, conditional on X+X_+,

v=X+β++ε,ε∣X+∼Nn(0,σ2In),n>p+1. v=X_+\beta_++\varepsilon,\qquad \varepsilon\mid X_+\sim N_n(0,\sigma^2 I_n),\qquad n>p+1.

Then β̂+−β+=(X+𝖳X+)−1X+𝖳ε\widehat\beta_+-\beta_+=(X_+^\mathsf{T}X_+)^{-1}X_+^\mathsf{T}\varepsilon is normal with covariance σ2(X+𝖳X+)−1\sigma^2(X_+^\mathsf{T}X_+)^{-1}. The residual is (I−H)ε(I-H)\varepsilon, where H=X+(X+𝖳X+)−1X+𝖳H=X_+(X_+^\mathsf{T}X_+)^{-1}X_+^\mathsf{T}. The projections HεH\varepsilon and (I−H)ε(I-H)\varepsilon are jointly normal and uncorrelated, hence independent. Because I−HI-H is an orthogonal projection of rank n−p−1n-p-1,

(n−p−1)s2σ2∼χn−p−12,s2=∥v−X+β̂+∥2n−p−1. \frac{(n-p-1)s^2}{\sigma^2}\sim\chi^2_{n-p-1}, \qquad s^2=\frac{\|v-X_+\widehat\beta_+\|^2}{n-p-1}.

Combining the independent normal and chi-square quantities gives a tn−p−1t_{n-p-1} pivot for each coefficient. The first row and column of X+𝖳X+X_+^\mathsf{T}X_+ correspond to the intercept, so slope jj occupies row and column j+1j+1. For slope j=1,…,pj=1,\ldots,p, a two-sided interval is

β̂j±tn−p−1,1−α/2s{(X+𝖳X+)−1}j+1,j+1. \widehat\beta_j\ \pm\ t_{n-p-1,1-\alpha/2} s\sqrt{\{(X_+^\mathsf{T}X_+)^{-1}\}_{j+1,j+1}}.

These are exact repeated-sampling statements for the stated Gaussian conditional model. Heterogeneous variances, dependent observations, model selection using these outcomes, and a misspecified mean need other arguments. The interval estimates a coefficient, not a new person’s response. A prediction interval also includes future residual variation. Estimating a mean and predicting an individual outcome therefore require different uncertainty calculations.

Likelihood for a fully observed binary outcome

Now change the outcome from a continuous measurement to an event indicator. The predictors can remain the same, but a Gaussian likelihood is no longer the proposed outcome model. We return to the Bernoulli distribution, allowing its probability to depend on each person’s predictors. Let YiY_i indicate a defined event within a specified horizon, completely observed for every person. Logistic regression specifies

πi=P(Yi=1∣Zi=zi)=expit(z+,i𝖳β+),expit(u)=11+e−u. \pi_i=P(Y_i=1\mid Z_i=z_i)=\operatorname{expit}(z_{+,i}^\mathsf{T}\beta_+), \qquad \operatorname{expit}(u)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-u}}.

With conditionally independent Bernoulli outcomes, the log likelihood is

ℓ(β+)=∑i[yiz+,i𝖳β+−log{1+exp(z+,i𝖳β+)}]. \ell(\beta_+)=\sum_i\left[y_i z_{+,i}^\mathsf{T}\beta_+ -\log\{1+\exp(z_{+,i}^\mathsf{T}\beta_+)\}\right].

Write π=(π1,…,πn)𝖳\pi=(\pi_1,\ldots,\pi_n)^\mathsf{T}. These are event probabilities; the unindexed pp remains the number of slopes. The score and negative Hessian are

U(β+)=X+𝖳(y−π),−∇2ℓ(β+)=X+𝖳WX+,W=diag{πi(1−πi)}. U(\beta_+)=X_+^\mathsf{T}(y-\pi),\qquad -\nabla^2\ell(\beta_+)=X_+^\mathsf{T}W X_+, \quad W=\operatorname{diag}\{\pi_i(1-\pi_i)\}.

The same Bernoulli likelihood yields the binary cross-entropy objective used when a more elaborate predictor produces probabilities. Calling the predictor a learned model does not change the endpoint or remove the likelihood’s assumptions. Optimizing cross-entropy estimates a probability function or approximation; a classification threshold is a subsequent action rule with a separate loss.

A Newton step uses (X+𝖳WX+)−1U(β+)(X_+^\mathsf{T}WX_+)^{-1}U(\beta_+). The conventional iteratively reweighted least-squares algorithm implements this update through a weighted working response. The code below instead minimizes the negative log likelihood directly and checks it against glm(). We compute log(1+eu)\log(1+e^u) as max(u,0)+log(1+e−|u|)\max(u,0)+\log(1+e^{-|u|}) to avoid unnecessary exponential overflow.

For a correctly specified, identifiable model with fixed dimension, suitable sampling conditions, and a finite interior estimate, likelihood curvature supports asymptotic normal inference. It does not give the exact tt pivot from the linear Gaussian model. Complete or quasi-complete separation can drive some coefficients toward infinity; a convergence message or a large fitted odds ratio is not a diagnosis of adequate information. Sparse outcomes and near separation can make ordinary Wald intervals poor approximations.

Two R implementations

The synthetic data contain 24 people. Age is centered at 60 and measured in decades; marker is a synthetic measured score. The code object X represents the augmented matrix X+X_+, so p <- ncol(X) - 1L counts only the two slopes; the residual degrees of freedom are 24−2−1=2124-2-1=21. response is a continuous measurement in arbitrary units, while event is a completely observed binary endpoint. These outcomes illustrate two distinct fitting objectives rather than two versions of the same response.

From first principles calculates the least-squares estimate, residual variance, tt interval, Bernoulli objective, score, and likelihood-curvature standard errors. Standard R workflow fits the identical design with stats::lm() and stats::glm(..., family = binomial(link = "logit")). All data, setup, and calculations are in this one block. Dependencies: base R and stats, included with R. [1], [2]

# Conditional regression: synthetic data, direct calculations, and standard fits.
# Dependencies: base R and stats (included in R).
options(width = 90)
clinical <- data.frame(
  age = c(44, 48, 51, 55, 58, 62, 66, 70, 46, 50, 54, 57,
          61, 65, 69, 73, 47, 52, 56, 60, 64, 68, 72, 76),
  marker = c(-1.2, 0.4, -0.6, 1.1, -0.3, 0.7, -1.0, 1.3,
             0.8, -0.9, 0.2, -1.1, 1.0, -0.4, 0.5, -0.7,
             -0.2, 1.2, -0.8, 0.3, -1.3, 0.9, -0.5, 0.6),
  event = c(0, 0, 1, 0, 0, 1, 0, 1, 0, 0, 1, 0,
            1, 0, 1, 1, 0, 1, 0, 1, 0, 1, 1, 1)
)
clinical$age10 <- (clinical$age - 60) / 10
clinical$response <- 110 + 8 * clinical$age10 + 5 * clinical$marker +
  c(2, -3, 1, 4, -2, 0, 3, -1, -4, 2, -1, 3,
    -2, 1, -3, 2, 4, -2, 0, 1, -4, 3, -1, 2)
X <- model.matrix(~ age10 + marker, data = clinical)
n <- nrow(X)
# p counts slopes; the first design column is the unpenalized intercept.
p <- ncol(X) - 1L

# From first principles: ordinary least squares and its conditional standard error.
beta.ls <- drop(solve(crossprod(X), crossprod(X, clinical$response)))
residual.ls <- clinical$response - drop(X %*% beta.ls)
s2 <- sum(residual.ls^2) / (n - p - 1L)
se.ls <- sqrt(diag(s2 * solve(crossprod(X))))
interval.ls <- cbind(lower = beta.ls - qt(.975, n - p - 1L) * se.ls,
                     upper = beta.ls + qt(.975, n - p - 1L) * se.ls)

# Standard R workflow: the same design, outcome, and intercept convention.
fit.ls <- lm(response ~ age10 + marker, data = clinical)
ols.table <- data.frame(term = colnames(X), direct = beta.ls,
                        lm = unname(coef(fit.ls)), SE = se.ls,
                        lower = interval.ls[, 1], upper = interval.ls[, 2])
cat("Linear response: coefficients and 95% t intervals\n")
print(ols.table, digits = 6, row.names = FALSE)
cat(sprintf("OLS residual SD: %.6f; residual degrees of freedom: %d\n\n",
            sqrt(s2), n - p - 1L))

# From first principles: a stable Bernoulli negative log likelihood and gradient.
softplus <- function(eta) pmax(eta, 0) + log1p(exp(-abs(eta)))
negative.loglik <- function(beta) {
  eta <- drop(X %*% beta)
  sum(softplus(eta) - clinical$event * eta)
}
gradient <- function(beta) {
  drop(crossprod(X, plogis(drop(X %*% beta)) - clinical$event))
}
fit.direct <- optim(rep(0, p + 1L), negative.loglik, gradient, method = "BFGS",
                    control = list(reltol = 1e-12, maxit = 1000))
beta.logit <- fit.direct$par
p.direct <- plogis(drop(X %*% beta.logit))
information <- crossprod(X, X * (p.direct * (1 - p.direct)))
se.logit <- sqrt(diag(solve(information)))

# Standard R workflow: binomial() uses the logit link by default.
fit.glm <- glm(event ~ age10 + marker, data = clinical,
               family = binomial(link = "logit"),
               control = glm.control(epsilon = 1e-10, maxit = 100))
logit.table <- data.frame(term = colnames(X), direct = beta.logit,
                          glm = unname(coef(fit.glm)), SE = se.logit)
cat("Binary event: coefficients and likelihood-curvature standard errors\n")
print(logit.table, digits = 6, row.names = FALSE)
new.person <- data.frame(age10 = .5, marker = .5)
new.X <- model.matrix(~ age10 + marker, data = new.person)
predicted.risk <- plogis(drop(new.X %*% beta.logit))
cat(sprintf("Risk at age 65, marker 0.5: %.6f\n", predicted.risk))
cat(sprintf("Age odds ratio per 10 years: %.6f\n", exp(beta.logit[2])))
cat(sprintf("Negative log likelihood: %.6f\n", negative.loglik(beta.logit)))
cat(sprintf("Maximum absolute score: %.3g\n", max(abs(gradient(beta.logit)))))
cat(sprintf("Maximum coefficient difference, optim versus glm: %.3g\n",
            max(abs(beta.logit - coef(fit.glm)))))

stopifnot(fit.direct$convergence == 0L, fit.glm$converged,
          max(abs(beta.ls - coef(fit.ls))) < 1e-10,
          max(abs(se.ls - coef(summary(fit.ls))[, 2])) < 1e-10,
          max(abs(interval.ls - confint(fit.ls))) < 1e-9,
          max(abs(beta.logit - coef(fit.glm))) < 1e-5,
          max(abs(se.logit - coef(summary(fit.glm))[, 2])) < 1e-5,
          max(abs(p.direct - fitted(fit.glm))) < 1e-6)

The linear age coefficient is 8.15239 response units per decade, conditional on the marker. Its interval is about 6.96370 to 9.34107 under the Gaussian conditional model. The coefficient describes the estimated magnitude on this response scale; the interval describes sampling uncertainty under the model. Neither supplies a criterion for whether that magnitude matters clinically. This is an association statement for the chosen adjustment set. The synthetic construction itself does not constitute evidence that real clinical residuals would satisfy that model.

In the binary model, the age coefficient 1.813717 corresponds to an odds ratio of about 6.13320 per decade at a fixed marker value. An odds ratio compares π/(1−π)\pi/(1-\pi), not π\pi; it is not a risk ratio. The fitted probability at age 65 and marker 0.5 is about 0.877831. This plug-in probability is distinct from the coefficient and from a decision to act at some threshold. It is an illustration, not a clinical recommendation.

The R vector p.direct contains the fitted event probabilities π̂i\widehat\pi_i; the scalar p remains the number of slopes. The direct optimizer and glm() differ by about 1.5×10−71.5\times10^{-7} in their largest coefficient difference. That reflects distinct numerical algorithms and stopping criteria. Both minimize the same negative log likelihood, and their fitted probabilities agree within the stated code tolerance. Linear intervals match confint(fit.ls) because both use the same Gaussian-model tt construction. We do not compare logistic Wald intervals to profile-likelihood intervals and demand artificial equality: those are different uncertainty methods.

What the fitted target can support

Before interpreting an estimate, examine whether the chosen relationship is plausible over the observed predictor range, whether a few observations dominate it, whether predictors are nearly redundant, and whether outcome observation matches the model. Residual-versus-fitted patterns can challenge a linear mean or common variance. A logistic model needs assessment of probability predictions, not a normality test of binary residuals. These checks assess what the conditional model supports. They do not by themselves establish that probability predictions remain accurate in a new population or justify a clinical action.

Conditional regression does not supply a causal identification strategy. Adjustment can remove confounding under an appropriate causal design, but it can also introduce bias through an unsuitable variable. In particular, holding a marker fixed in a fitted regression is a mathematical comparison, not necessarily an intervention available in clinical practice.

Exercises

  1. The intercept consumes a degree of freedom. Conditional on a fixed full-rank design X+∈ℝn×(p+1)X_+\in\mathbb R^{n\times(p+1)} whose first column is ones, suppose y=X+β++εy=X_+\beta_++\varepsilon, ε∼Nn(0,σ2In)\varepsilon\sim N_n(0,\sigma^2I_n), n>p+1n>p+1. State why the residual degrees of freedom are n−p−1n-p-1, and why the interval is not a prediction interval.

  2. Changing units preserves the fitted model. In a logistic model Pr(Y=1∣a,m)=expit{β0+βa(a−60)/10+βmm}\Pr(Y=1\mid a,m)=\operatorname{expit}\{\beta_0+\beta_a(a-60)/10+\beta_m m\}, age aa is measured in years and mm is a marker held fixed in the comparison. Explain why the odds ratio is not generally that risk ratio or a causal intervention effect.

Connections

The mathematical presentation and synthetic example are original teaching developments using standard linear-model and likelihood theory. The later chapters on confidence sets and hypothesis testing develop the interpretation and repeated-sampling justification of these uncertainty statements.

The example has given us coefficient estimates, uncertainty calculations and fitted probabilities, each answering a different question. We have fitted the models, but we still need a criterion for comparing estimates. In the next chapter, we return to a simpler Bernoulli experiment and make that criterion explicit through loss. The smaller model lets us examine a bias–variance tradeoff exactly before regularization introduces the same issue in a larger regression.
