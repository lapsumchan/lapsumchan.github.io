Chapter 9. Empirical Bayes and the geometry of shrinkage

Why estimate related quantities together? Suppose several laboratories report standardized estimates of different effects. Each estimate has appreciable sampling error, and there is reason to expect many effects to lie near a shared center. An analysis of each laboratory in isolation discards this structure; forcing all effects to be equal discards their differences. Partial pooling uses evidence about their dispersion to choose an intermediate action. To evaluate pooling, we need to ask whether it improves the estimates or decisions for which these quantities will be used; a smoother graph alone cannot answer that question.

Chapter 8 derived posterior means under specified priors, while Chapter 4 compared estimators through their loss and risk. We now bring these calculations to a vector of related means. A hierarchical model gives a pooling rule; empirical Bayes estimates its strength from the vector; a frequentist risk identity lets us assess one precisely defined shrinkage procedure. The guarantee concerns total squared error across the vector, not improvement for every coordinate.

9.1 A vector experiment and an explicit loss

Throughout the main argument, observe one vector X∼Nd(θ,Id),θ∈ℝd,L(θ,a)=∥a−θ∥2=∑j=1d(aj−θj)2.X\sim N_d(\theta,I_d),\qquad \theta\in\mathbb R^d, \qquad L(\theta,a)=\|a-\theta\|^2=\sum_{j=1}^d(a_j-\theta_j)^2. \label{i12-experiment}(9.1) The integer dd counts mean coordinates, not repeated observations on one mean. Conditional on the fixed vector θ\theta, the coordinates are independent with known variance one. A raw estimate with a common known standard error can be divided by that standard error to reach this scale. Different standard errors, dependence, missing coordinates, or uncertainty about the variances require a different argument. Standardization alone does not make correlated measurements independent.

The estimator δ0(X)=X\delta_0(X)=X has risk R(θ,δ0)=dR(\theta,\delta_0)=d for every θ\theta: each coordinate contributes one. An estimator can reduce variance by moving observations toward a center, but introduces bias if true effects lie away from that center. With a fixed weight ww and fixed center zero, δw(X)=wX\delta_w(X)=wX has R(θ,δw)=w2d+(1−w)2∥θ∥2.R(\theta,\delta_w)=w^2d+(1-w)^2\|\theta\|^2. \label{i12-fixed-weight-risk}(9.2) For w<1w<1, this risk eventually exceeds dd as the signal grows. An appealing prior-average performance therefore does not prove uniform improvement at every fixed parameter. The James–Stein construction adapts the weight to the observed vector and escapes this particular fixed-weight limitation.

Total squared loss expresses equal importance of all standardized coordinates. If one coordinate is scientifically primary, or actions have different consequences, that loss may be inappropriate. Loss is part of the problem specification, as in Chapter 4; a theorem for one loss cannot be transferred silently to another.

9.2 Normal–normal pooling from first principles

For a working hierarchical model, suppose independently θj∣μ,τ2∼N(μ,τ2),Xj∣θj∼N(θj,1),τ2>0.\theta_j\mid\mu,\tau^2\sim N(\mu,\tau^2),\qquad X_j\mid\theta_j\sim N(\theta_j,1),\qquad \tau^2>0. \label{i12-hierarchy}(9.3) The exchangeability assumption assigns the same prior distribution to coordinates whose labels convey no further information in this model. It is a substantive modeling choice. Measurements belonging to different biological pathways, populations, or exposure scales may need distinct centers or covariates rather than one common pool.

Hold μ,τ2\mu,\tau^2 fixed initially. The posterior kernel for one coordinate is exp[−12{(xj−θj)2+(θj−μ)2τ2}].\exp\left[-\frac12\left\{(x_j-\theta_j)^2+ \frac{(\theta_j-\mu)^2}{\tau^2}\right\}\right]. Completing the square, exactly as in Chapter 8, gives θj∣Xj=xj,μ,τ2∼N(μ+w(xj−μ),w),w=τ21+τ2.\theta_j\mid X_j=x_j,\mu,\tau^2 \sim N\left(\mu+w(x_j-\mu),w\right), \qquad w=\frac{\tau^2}{1+\tau^2}. \label{i12-normal-posterior}(9.4) The posterior mean is the squared-error Bayes action. Small between-coordinate variance yields stronger pooling, while large τ2\tau^2 makes ww approach one. At the boundary τ2=0\tau^2=0, the prior is degenerate at μ\mu, and the limiting posterior also places all probability there. This boundary is a different model component rather than a normal density with positive variance.

The marginal distribution follows without an integration trick. Write Xj=μ+Uj+εjX_j=\mu+U_j+\varepsilon_j, where Uj∼N(0,τ2)U_j\sim N(0,\tau^2) and εj∼N(0,1)\varepsilon_j\sim N(0,1) are independent. Their sum is normal with variance τ2+1\tau^2+1: Xj∣μ,τ2∼N(μ,1+τ2),j=1,…,d.X_j\mid\mu,\tau^2\sim N(\mu,1+\tau^2),\quad j=1,\ldots,d. \label{i12-marginal}(9.5) The usefulness of the normal hierarchy and empirical-Bayes construction depends on whether a common marginal model adequately represents these coordinates; estimating its parameters does not remove that modeling choice.

Under the stated prior, the Bayes risk of the posterior mean is dw=dτ2/(1+τ2)dw=d\tau^2/(1+\tau^2), because the conditional posterior variance is ww in every coordinate. This is an average over the prior distribution of θ\theta, distinct from fixed-θ\theta risk in Equation 9.2. Retaining the subscript or explicitly stating the averaging distribution prevents an apparent contradiction between those two calculations.

9.3 Estimating the pooling strength

Empirical Bayes estimates hyperparameters from the marginal distribution, then inserts those estimates into the posterior rule. Define X¯=d−1∑jXj\overline X=d^{-1}\sum_jX_j and Sc=∑j(Xj−X¯)2S_c=\sum_j(X_j-\overline X)^2. Under Equation 9.5, 𝔼X¯=μ\mathbb{E}\overline X=\mu and 𝔼Sc=(d−1)(1+τ2)\mathbb{E}S_c=(d-1)(1+\tau^2). For d≥2d\ge2, a constrained moment estimate is μ̂=X¯,τ̂mom2=max{0,Scd−1−1}.\widehat\mu=\overline X,\qquad \widehat\tau^2_{\mathrm{mom}} =\max\left\{0,\frac{S_c}{d-1}-1\right\}. \label{i12-moment}(9.6) The truncation enforces the variance parameter’s nonnegative domain; the resulting estimate is not unbiased. A negative untruncated value means the observed dispersion is less than the known noise variance, not that a negative variance is meaningful.

Maximum marginal likelihood uses a different denominator. Put v=1+τ2≥1v=1+\tau^2\ge1. Apart from constants, ℓ(μ,v)=−d2logv−12v∑j(xj−μ)2.\ell(\mu,v)=-\frac d2\log v-\frac1{2v}\sum_j(x_j-\mu)^2. For every vv, the maximizer in μ\mu is x¯\overline x. Differentiating the profile likelihood in vv gives the unconstrained optimizer Sc/dS_c/d. Hence τ̂ML2=max{0,Sc/d−1},θ̂jEB=μ̂+τ̂21+τ̂2(Xj−μ̂).\widehat\tau^2_{\mathrm{ML}}=\max\{0,S_c/d-1\},\qquad \widehat\theta^{\mathrm{EB}}_j =\widehat\mu+\frac{\widehat\tau^2}{1+\widehat\tau^2} (X_j-\widehat\mu). \label{i12-marginal-mle}(9.7) Moment and likelihood fitting need not yield the same amount of pooling, especially with few coordinates. If μ\mu is fixed externally instead, replace ScS_c by Sμ=∑j(Xj−μ)2S_\mu=\sum_j(X_j-\mu)^2; both the marginal variance moment and likelihood estimators use Sμ/dS_\mu/d. Estimating the center costs a degree of freedom in the unbiased moment calculation.

Consider a new synthetic vector of standardized observations (2.4,1.1,−0.3,1.6,0.2,−0.6,1.9,0.8)(2.4,1.1,-0.3,1.6,0.2,-0.6,1.9,0.8). These eight coordinates illustrate pooling of different means; they are not repeated observations on the single mean from the previous chapter. Here x¯=0.8875\overline x=0.8875 and Sc=7.96875S_c=7.96875. The moment variance estimate is 0.13839290.1383929 and its weight 0.12156860.1215686; the marginal likelihood estimate is zero and therefore pools all eight coordinates at the fitted center. The two answers are close scientifically in their strong pooling, but materially different numerically at the model boundary.

js <- function(x, positive = FALSE) {
  d <- ncol(x)
  if (d < 3L) stop("Fixed-center James-Stein comparison requires d >= 3")
  s <- rowSums(x^2)
  weight <- 1 - (d - 2) / s
  weight[s == 0] <- 0 # Definition at the probability-zero origin.
  if (positive) weight <- pmax(0, weight)
  x * weight
}

# Q ~ noncentral chi-square(d, lambda).  Its Poisson mixture gives E(1/Q).
reciprocal.exact <- function(d, lambda) {
  stopifnot(d > 2, lambda >= 0)
  if (lambda == 0) return(1 / (d - 2))
  top <- qpois(1 - 1e-13, lambda / 2)
  j <- 0:top
  sum(dpois(j, lambda / 2) / (d + 2 * j - 2))
}
risk.exact <- function(d, magnitude) {
  d - (d - 2)^2 * reciprocal.exact(d, magnitude^2)
}
stopifnot(abs(risk.exact(3, 0) - 2) < 1e-12,
          abs(risk.exact(20, 0) - 2) < 1e-12)
# Independent Laplace-transform integral for noncentral cases.
integral.checks <- expand.grid(d = c(3, 5, 10, 20), magnitude = c(0, 2, 8))
integral.checks$mixture <- mapply(function(d, m) reciprocal.exact(d, m^2),
                                 integral.checks$d, integral.checks$magnitude)
integral.checks$integral <- mapply(function(d, m) {
  integrate(function(t) (1 + 2*t)^(-d/2) * exp(-m^2*t/(1+2*t)),
            0, Inf, rel.tol = 1e-9, subdivisions = 1000)$value
}, integral.checks$d, integral.checks$magnitude)
stopifnot(max(abs(integral.checks$mixture-integral.checks$integral)) < 2e-8)
write.csv(integral.checks, "generated/ch12-exact-checks.csv", row.names = FALSE)

                      -Inf, Inf, rel.tol=1e-10)$value/normalizer

# Paired comparison: all procedures see each exact same noise vector.

grid <- expand.grid(d = c(5, 10, 20), magnitude = c(0, 1, 2, 4, 8, 16))
rows <- vector("list", nrow(grid))
for (k in seq_len(nrow(grid))) {

# 0.8875000, 7.9687500, 0.1383929, 0.1215686

The fitted center is not a known external target. With τ2\tau^2 fixed but an improper flat prior on μ\mu, the hierarchy gives μ∣X∼N(X¯,(1+τ2)/d)\mu\mid X\sim N(\overline X,(1+\tau^2)/d). Integrating over this center uncertainty yields marginal posterior variance for each θj\theta_j equal to w+(1−w)/dw+(1-w)/d, rather than ww. This follows from total variance: conditional variance is ww and conditional mean depends on μ\mu through coefficient 1−w1-w. At τ2=0\tau^2=0, the remaining variance is 1/d1/d; the common mean is still uncertain. A plug-in zero posterior variance would erase that uncertainty. This illustrative calculation conditions on known τ2\tau^2; estimating the latter adds another uncertainty layer.

Center uncertainty also induces posterior dependence. With τ2\tau^2 known, the coordinates are conditionally independent given X,μX,\mu, but for j≠kj\ne k the law of total covariance gives Cov(θj,θk∣X)=(1−w)2Var(μ∣X)=1−wd.\operatorname{Cov}(\theta_j,\theta_k\mid X) =(1-w)^2\operatorname{Var}(\mu\mid X)=\frac{1-w}{d}. \label{i12-center-covariance}(9.8) Thus the posterior covariance matrix is wId+(1−w)𝟏𝟏T/dwI_d+(1-w)\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^T/d. At τ2=0\tau^2=0, all coordinates are the same uncertain mean; their differences have zero posterior variance, while each coordinate has variance 1/d1/d. Distinguishing uncertainty about a contrast from uncertainty about an absolute level explains why complete pooling need not imply precise knowledge of every effect.

9.4 Stein’s identity and an unbiased estimate of risk

The hierarchy has supplied a shrinkage rule. To evaluate its repeated-sampling performance, we now return to fixed θ\theta in Equation 9.1; no prior is assumed true. For an appropriate scalar function gjg_j of the vector, normal integration by parts gives 𝔼θ{(Xj−θj)gj(X)}=𝔼θ{∂gj(X)∂xj}.\mathbb{E}_\theta\{(X_j-\theta_j)g_j(X)\} =\mathbb{E}_\theta\left\{\frac{\partial g_j(X)}{\partial x_j}\right\}. \label{i12-stein}(9.9) To see the sign and boundary condition, the normal density satisfies ∂fθ(x)/∂xj=−(xj−θj)fθ(x)\partial f_\theta(x)/\partial x_j=-(x_j-\theta_j)f_\theta(x). Integrate −gj∂fθ/∂xj-g_j\partial f_\theta/\partial x_j over xjx_j; a vanishing boundary term leaves (∂gj/∂xj)fθ(\partial g_j/\partial x_j)f_\theta. Then integrate the other coordinates. Sufficient conditions include differentiability, integrable terms, and decay making these operations valid. Singular functions require a separate justification, supplied below.

Write any suitably regular estimator as δ(X)=X+g(X)\delta(X)=X+g(X). Expanding its total error and applying Equation 9.9 coordinatewise yields R(θ,δ)=d+𝔼θ{∥g(X)∥2+2divg(X)},divg=∑j=1d∂gj∂xj.R(\theta,\delta) =d+\mathbb{E}_\theta\{\|g(X)\|^2+2\operatorname{div}g(X)\}, \quad \operatorname{div}g=\sum_{j=1}^d\frac{\partial g_j}{\partial x_j}. \label{i12-sure}(9.10) Thus the realized expression d+∥g(X)∥2+2divg(X)d+\|g(X)\|^2+2\operatorname{div}g(X) is Stein’s unbiased risk estimate, or SURE. It estimates repeated-sampling total risk, not the unobserved error on this particular vector. It can be negative even though risk is nonnegative. Further, selecting a procedure by minimizing its observed SURE and reporting that same minimum as an unbiased assessment ignores selection. The identity evaluates a specified rule; it does not automatically validate unrestricted tuning after inspection.

A fixed shrinkage weight provides a check on the identity before we use a singular function. If δw=wX\delta_w=wX, then g(x)=(w−1)xg(x)=(w-1)x and divg=d(w−1)\operatorname{div}g=d(w-1). SURE becomes d+(w−1)2∥X∥2+2d(w−1).d+(w-1)^2\|X\|^2+2d(w-1). Its expectation is d+(w−1)2(d+∥θ∥2)+2d(w−1)d+(w-1)^2(d+\|\theta\|^2)+2d(w-1), which simplifies to w2d+(1−w)2∥θ∥2w^2d+(1-w)^2\|\theta\|^2, recovering Equation (9.2). This calculation makes the distinction concrete: SURE is a data-based estimate of a specified procedure’s risk, and its expected value is the analytic risk curve.

For a constant aa, take g(x)=−ax/∥x∥2g(x)=-ax/\|x\|^2 away from zero and assign any finite value at zero, which has probability zero. Put S=∥x∥2S=\|x\|^2. Direct differentiation gives ∥g(x)∥2=a2S,divg(x)=−adS+2a∑jxj2S2=−a(d−2)S.\|g(x)\|^2=\frac{a^2}{S},\qquad \operatorname{div}g(x) =-\frac{ad}{S}+\frac{2a\sum_jx_j^2}{S^2} =-\frac{a(d-2)}{S}. \label{i12-divergence}(9.11) The coefficient d−2d-2 comes from the geometry of this vector derivative. Replacing it with dd, or differentiating a data-dependent shrinkage factor as if it were constant, produces the wrong risk calculation.

For d>2d>2, 𝔼θ(S−1)\mathbb{E}_\theta(S^{-1}) is finite. Near the origin a bounded normal density and spherical volume element produce an integral proportional to ∫0ϵrd−3dr\int_0^\epsilon r^{d-3}\,dr; outside a ball, S−1S^{-1} is bounded. One rigorous route is to first use gε(x)=−ax/(S+ε)g_\varepsilon(x)=-ax/(S+\varepsilon), then let ε\varepsilon decrease to zero. Its squared norm is bounded by a2/Sa^2/S, and the absolute derivatives by 3|a|/S3|a|/S; integrability permits passage to the limit. This establishes the identity for this singular rule, rather than assuming ordinary integration by parts automatically covers it.

9.5 James–Stein risk and its exact scope

Substitution into SURE gives R(θ,δa)=d+{a2−2a(d−2)}𝔼θ(S−1),δa(X)=(1−a∥X∥2)X.R(\theta,\delta_a)=d+ \{a^2-2a(d-2)\}\mathbb{E}_\theta(S^{-1}), \qquad \delta_a(X)=\left(1-\frac a{\|X\|^2}\right)X. \label{i12-general-risk}(9.12) For d≥3d\ge3 and 0<a<2(d−2)0<a<2(d-2), the coefficient is negative and the expectation positive: this rule has smaller risk than XX for every fixed θ\theta. Within this constant-aa family, the quadratic coefficient is minimized at a=d−2a=d-2.

Theorem 9.1 (Fixed-center James–Stein improvement). Under Equation 9.1, for d≥3d\ge3, the estimator δJS(X)=(1−d−2∥X∥2)X\delta_{\mathrm{JS}}(X)=\left(1-\frac{d-2}{\|X\|^2}\right)X \label{i12-js-rule}(9.13) has total squared-error risk R(θ,δJS)=d−(d−2)2𝔼θ{∥X∥−2}<d.R(\theta,\delta_{\mathrm{JS}}) =d-(d-2)^2\mathbb{E}_\theta\{\|X\|^{-2}\}<d. \label{i12-js-risk}(9.14) The value assigned at X=0X=0 does not change the risk.

Proof. Equations 9.10–9.12, including their integrability justification, apply with a=d−2a=d-2. The finite expectation of a strictly positive random variable is positive, establishing strict improvement. ◻

At θ=0\theta=0, S∼χd2S\sim\chi^2_d and 𝔼(1/S)=1/(d−2)\mathbb{E}(1/S)=1/(d-2), so the risk is exactly two regardless of dimension d≥3d\ge3. This follows by integrating the chi-square density, or by its gamma moment formula. The result does not claim zero risk at the origin; the shrinkage rule remains noisy and can reverse an observed coordinate’s sign.

For any fixed external center cc, translation gives the analogous rule c+{1−(d−2)/∥X−c∥2}(X−c)c+\{1-(d-2)/\|X-c\|^2\}(X-c) and the same dominance conclusion. Replacing cc by X¯\overline X is not the same proof. The residual vector then lives in a (d−1)(d-1)-dimensional subspace, and the estimated center has its own error. The centered construction uses a d−3d-3 coefficient for this reason; simply using d−2d-2 with a fitted center misapplies the fixed-center theorem. It need not produce a bad estimator, but requires its own proof. In an orthonormal coordinate system with first direction d−1/2(1,…,1)d^{-1/2}(1,\ldots,1), estimating the unshrunk center direction contributes risk one. Shrinking the remaining d−1d-1 normal coordinates with coefficient aa contributes (d−1)+{a2−2a(d−3)}𝔼θ(Sc−1).(d-1)+\{a^2-2a(d-3)\}\mathbb{E}_\theta(S_c^{-1}). For d≥4d\ge4, their sum is below dd when 0<a<2(d−3)0<a<2(d-3), minimized within this family at a=d−3a=d-3. This residual-space argument distinguishes an externally fixed center from an estimated one while retaining the latter’s risk.

The positive-part modification replaces the factor by max{0,1−(d−2)/S}\max\{0,1-(d-2)/S\}, eliminating sign reversal. It is included in the numerical comparison as a defined alternative. Its additional domination result requires a further argument not proved here; we do not infer that theorem from a few simulated parameter values. Similarly, empirical-Bayes fitting toward an estimated center is not covered by the displayed theorem merely because both methods shrink.

The theorem also illustrates a worst-case risk comparison. Product-normal proper priors θ∼Nd(0,τ2Id)\theta\sim N_d(0,\tau^2I_d) have optimal Bayes risk dτ2/(1+τ2)d\tau^2/(1+\tau^2), approaching dd as τ2→∞\tau^2\to\infty. For any proper prior, the smallest prior-average risk is a lower bound on the smallest worst-case risk. Taking the limit of these lower bounds shows that the minimax value in this experiment is dd, attained by XX. James–Stein risk is at most dd everywhere, so this rule is minimax too, despite strictly improving on XX at every finite θ\theta. The raw vector is minimax and inadmissible. Strict pointwise dominance and equal supremum risk can coexist; the supremum need not be attained at a finite parameter.

9.6 An executable risk audit and what it can reveal

At fixed θ\theta, simulate X=θ+ZX=\theta+Z with independent standard normal coordinates and calculate the losses of both rules on the same vector. Pairing removes much of the shared noise from their difference. A Monte Carlo standard error for the estimated difference is the sample standard deviation of the paired differences divided by the square root of the number of independent repetitions.

This excerpt shows a step inside the preceding complete R example; it uses that block’s data and setup.

set.seed(802312)
d <- 5; B <- 100000; theta <- c(4,rep(0,d-1))
Z <- matrix(rnorm(B*d),nrow=B)
X <- sweep(Z,2,theta,"+")
S <- rowSums(X^2)
estimate <- X*(1-(d-2)/S)
loss.x <- rowSums(Z^2)
loss.js <- rowSums(sweep(estimate,2,theta,"-")^2)
difference <- loss.js-loss.x
c(difference=mean(difference),mcse=sd(difference)/sqrt(B))

There is a computational caution at small dimensions. At the origin, squared error for the ordinary rule has a singular contribution proportional to 1/S1/S. Its expectation exists for d>2d>2, but its second moment fails for d=3,4d=3,4. A finite simulated sample standard deviation in those dimensions does not establish the finite-variance central-limit justification of the usual Monte Carlo standard error. The paired simulation grid therefore starts at d=5d=5; exact risk curves still include d=3d=3. A mathematical integrability condition can change how a computational result should be assessed.

Finally examine coordinate risks. A separate 400,000-repetition experiment at θ=(4,0,0,0,0)\theta=(4,0,0,0,0) finds James–Stein risk near 1.59 for coordinate one, versus 1.00 for its raw observation; the other coordinates have risks near 0.72 each. Their improvements outweigh the first coordinate’s deterioration in total loss. This is a counterexample to the assertion that the rule helps every individual component. Rotational symmetry makes total risk depend only on ∥θ∥\|\theta\|, but does not make coordinate risks identical when the coordinates have different true means.

9.7 Uncertainty, model checking, and scientific interpretation

Plug-in empirical-Bayes posterior summaries condition on fitted hyperparameters as though they were known. Their intervals are not automatically calibrated either under fixed-θ\theta repetition or under the full fitted hierarchy. Boundary variance estimates are especially revealing: complete pooling can return zero conditional posterior variance while the fitted common center remains uncertain. A full hierarchical analysis puts a prior on hyperparameters and integrates their posterior distribution; a carefully designed bootstrap refits hyperparameters in each simulated dataset. These are additional methods, not interchangeable justifications for a plug-in interval.

Before choosing pooling, examine the observation variances, dependence, comparability of coordinates, and plausibility of a common distribution. Posterior predictive checks can compare the observed dispersion or tail behavior with replicated vectors, but fitting and checking the same summary creates limitations described in Chapter 8. Subgroup patterns discarded by a single normal marginal model cannot be recovered by tuning its variance. Unequal scientific consequences require a corresponding loss, and an aggregate accuracy gain does not establish calibrated uncertainty or better threshold decisions.

For genetic effect estimates or regression coefficients, we first need their sampling covariance, standard errors, and any selection that produced the vector. These features determine whether a pooling model is defensible and which risk comparison is relevant. The next chapter returns to a regression design: penalties can reduce variability there too, but coefficient uncertainty requires an argument beyond a squared-error improvement.
