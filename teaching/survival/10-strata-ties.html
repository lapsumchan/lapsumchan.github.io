Chapter 8. Stratification and tied event times

8.1 Which part of the model should be shared?

Chapter 7 separated a relative-hazard parameter from an unspecified baseline hazard. That flexibility did not remove every restriction. A standard Cox model still says that one baseline curve, multiplied by fixed covariate factors, describes everyone. In a study spanning hospitals, disease stages, or enrollment eras, this may be more structure than the scientific question requires. The recording clock raises a separate question: several events can share one recorded time even when the underlying times are continuous.

These issues enter different parts of the argument. Stratification changes which people are compared in a risk set and which baseline hazards are shared. A tie method changes how a set of simultaneous recorded failures contributes to the partial likelihood. Both choices affect the statistical comparison. We derive their contributions to the partial likelihood before fitting models, then ask what the fitted coefficients and baseline curves tell us.

The starting assumptions remain independent subjects, correctly specified event and time-origin definitions, and noninformative censoring conditional on the covariates and stratum used in the model. Relevant groups must have overlap in covariates and observable follow-up through the reporting horizon. The new baseline flexibility does not make informative loss to follow-up disappear.

8.2 A baseline curve for each stratum

Let si∈{1,…,S}s_i\in\{1,\ldots,S\} be a known baseline stratum; here SS counts strata, whereas S(t)S(t) denotes survival. We write each covariate profile ziz_i and its coefficient vector β\beta as matching pp-dimensional column vectors. The design matrix has one row ziTz_i^T per person, and a separate intercept is absorbed into the baseline hazard. The stratified proportional-hazards model is λi(t∣zi,si=s)=λ0s(t)exp(βTzi).\lambda_i(t\mid z_i,s_i=s)=\lambda_{0s}(t)\exp(\beta^Tz_i). \label{s10-model}(8.1) There is no assumed proportionality between λ0s\lambda_{0s} and λ0r\lambda_{0r} for different strata. The coefficient vector β\beta is shared. Thus the model allows different underlying time patterns while retaining one within-stratum covariate association.

Compare this with adding stratum indicators: λi(t)=λ0(t)exp{βTzi+αsi}.\lambda_i(t)=\lambda_0(t)\exp\{\beta^Tz_i+\alpha_{s_i}\}. The indicator model restricts all stratum baseline hazards to be constant multiples of one curve. Stratification removes that restriction but also removes a single estimated hazard ratio for changing stratum. Its baseline functions can cross. They can have different peaks or growth rates. A fitted β\beta still concerns comparisons within the same stratum and at the same time.

A variable constant within each stratum cannot have an identifiable separate coefficient in (8.1). For example, exp(αs)\exp(\alpha_s) can always be absorbed into λ0s\lambda_{0s}. Stratifying on a hospital therefore allows hospital-specific baseline curves, but it does not estimate a hospital coefficient alongside those unrestricted curves. A continuous age effect can be fitted within broad age strata because age still varies inside them. This is different from adding a redundant coefficient for the very category used to define a stratum.

8.3 Partial likelihood within the appropriate risk sets

At an event time tsjt_{sj} in stratum ss, define ℛsj={i:si=s,Xi≥tsj}.\mathcal R_{sj}=\{i:s_i=s,\ X_i\geq t_{sj}\}. For now assume distinct event times within a stratum, and denote the event subject by isji_{sj}. Conditional on one event in this risk set, its identity has probability exp(βTzisj)∑k∈ℛsjexp(βTzk).\frac{\exp(\beta^Tz_{i_{sj}})} {\sum_{k\in\mathcal R_{sj}}\exp(\beta^Tz_k)}. \label{s10-risk-probability}(8.2) The baseline cancels because it is common to the members of this risk set. It would not cancel if the denominator mixed subjects from different strata with different baseline hazards. Consequently the stratified partial likelihood multiplies within-stratum factors: Lstr(β)=∏s∏jexp(βTzisj)∑k∈ℛsjexp(βTzk).L_{\mathrm{str}}(\beta)= \prod_s\prod_j \frac{\exp(\beta^Tz_{i_{sj}})} {\sum_{k\in\mathcal R_{sj}}\exp(\beta^Tz_k)}. \label{s10-partial}(8.3) The construction compares locally, then adds evidence across strata. It does not pretend that the marginal populations have identical composition.

Write z¯sj(β)\overline z_{sj}(\beta) for the exponentially weighted mean in ℛsj\mathcal R_{sj}, and Vsj(β)V_{sj}(\beta) for its weighted covariance. Differentiating the log likelihood gives Ustr(β)=∑s∑j{zisj−z¯sj(β)},Istr(β)=∑s∑jVsj(β).U_{\mathrm{str}}(\beta)=\sum_s\sum_j \{z_{i_{sj}}-\overline z_{sj}(\beta)\}, \qquad I_{\mathrm{str}}(\beta)=\sum_s\sum_j V_{sj}(\beta). \label{s10-score}(8.4) The coefficient is identified by variation within informative risk sets. A stratum with no events supplies no event factor. A risk set in which a covariate is constant supplies no information about that covariate. Dividing a study into many tiny strata can therefore lose information, even when the baseline flexibility is scientifically defensible.

For example, suppose one event occurs in each of two strata. Stratum A has three group-0 and one group-1 participant at risk, and its event is in group 1. Stratum B has one group-0 and three group-1 participants at risk, and its event is in group 0. For r=eβr=e^\beta, their contribution is Lstr(r)=r3+r11+3r,U(β)=1−r3+r−3r1+3r.L_{\mathrm{str}}(r)=\frac{r}{3+r}\frac{1}{1+3r},\qquad U(\beta)=1-\frac{r}{3+r}-\frac{3r}{1+3r}. At r=1r=1 the two score increments are 3/43/4 and −3/4-3/4, and their information increments are both 3/163/16. The fitted common coefficient is zero, but the two local comparisons are not identical. Pooling all eight participants into each denominator would replace the two weighted group fractions by r/(1+r)r/(1+r); its information at zero would be 1/21/2 rather than 3/83/8. Mixing strata also prevents cancellation of a common baseline when their baseline hazards differ. The full implementation therefore subsets the risk set before it calculates weights.

8.4 Baseline estimation and interpretation after stratification

With β̂\widehat\beta fitted, the Breslow baseline estimator is now stratum-specific: Ĥ0s(t)=∑tsj≤tdsj∑k∈ℛsjexp(β̂Tzk).\widehat H_{0s}(t)= \sum_{t_{sj}\leq t} \frac{d_{sj}}{\sum_{k\in\mathcal R_{sj}}\exp(\widehat\beta^Tz_k)}. \label{s10-baseline}(8.5) Each denominator belongs to the same stratum as its numerator. An absolute survival prediction needs both a covariate profile and a stratum: Ŝ(t∣z,s)=exp{−Ĥ0s(t)exp(β̂Tz)}.\widehat S(t\mid z,s)= \exp\{-\widehat H_{0s}(t)\exp(\widehat\beta^Tz)\}. \label{s10-survival}(8.6) The shared hazard ratio acts on a different baseline in each stratum. Comparing predicted risks across strata therefore combines baseline differences with covariate associations.

Standardizing stratum-specific risks to a chosen distribution is possible, but the target weights must be stated. For weights asa_s summing to one, ∑sas[1−Ŝ(t∣z,s)]\sum_s a_s[1-\widehat S(t\mid z,s)] is a standardized descriptive risk for that mixture. It is not the prediction for an unspecified new hospital whose baseline curve was never observed. Assigning a new stratum requires additional modeling or transport assumptions.

A shared β\beta is also an assumption. To allow a group effect to differ between two strata, include zz and z𝟏{s=B}z\mathbf{1}\{s=B\} while retaining separate baselines. The coefficient of the interaction is a difference between within-stratum log hazard ratios. It can be tested through an appropriate nested-model comparison if prespecified and adequately supported. Allowing stratum-specific baselines alone does not allow stratum-specific covariate slopes.

8.4.1 A conditional hazard ratio need not survive averaging

Even if the same β\beta holds in every stratum, the marginal hazard need not have a constant group ratio. For common target weights asa_s and a scalar group coefficient, standardize survival as Smix(t∣z)=∑sasexp{−H0s(t)eβz}.S_{\mathrm{mix}}(t\mid z)=\sum_sa_s \exp\{-H_{0s}(t)e^{\beta z}\}. Differentiating its negative logarithm gives λmix(t∣z)=eβz∑sasλ0s(t)Ss(t∣z)∑sasSs(t∣z).\lambda_{\mathrm{mix}}(t\mid z)=e^{\beta z} \frac{\sum_sa_s\lambda_{0s}(t)S_s(t\mid z)} {\sum_sa_sS_s(t\mid z)}. \label{s10-mixture-hazard}(8.7) The survivors’ stratum weights depend on both time and group because survival differs across strata. The weighted baseline rate in this fraction therefore need not cancel between z=1z=1 and z=0z=0. A conditional hazard ratio eβe^\beta can coexist with a time-varying marginal hazard ratio, even before considering different initial group compositions.

We have changed the target by averaging. A standardized risk contrast describes the chosen population mixture, while the common coefficient describes a within-stratum hazard comparison. Their different values can follow the same fitted model. Reporting each with its target makes the comparison interpretable.

8.5 A connected experiment with different baseline shapes

The complete R example reuses the 600 baseline covariate profiles from Chapter 6. It leaves that chapter’s outcomes intact. For a separate, explicitly synthetic experiment it assigns a center and generates new outcomes. Center B is more frequent among z=1z=1 profiles, so a pooled comparison mixes group association with center composition.

The generating baseline cumulative hazards are H0A(t)=(t/10)1.2,H0B(t)=(t/6)2,H_{0A}(t)=(t/10)^{1.2},\qquad H_{0B}(t)=(t/6)^2, and the shared coefficients are βz=0.5\beta_z=0.5, βage=0.3\beta_{\mathrm{age}}=0.3, with age measured in decades from 50. Their baseline hazard ratio changes with t0.8t^{0.8}; a constant center indicator cannot reproduce both shapes. Event times are obtained by inverting H0s(T)exp(βTz)=EH_{0s}(T)\exp(\beta^Tz)=E, where EE is unit exponential. Independent exponential censoring and administrative closure at month 10 complete the observation model.

# Complete R example. Synthetic data; no external files are required.

biomedical.cohort <- function(n, seed) {
  old.seed <- if (exists(".Random.seed", envir=.GlobalEnv)) get(".Random.seed", envir=.GlobalEnv) else NULL
  on.exit(if (is.null(old.seed)) {if (exists(".Random.seed", envir=.GlobalEnv)) rm(".Random.seed",envir=.GlobalEnv)} else assign(".Random.seed",old.seed,envir=.GlobalEnv))
  set.seed(seed)
  z <- rbinom(n,1,.45); age <- runif(n,35,70); age10 <- (age-50)/10
  eta <- log(9)-.35*z-.20*age10
  event.time <- exp(eta)*(-log(runif(n)))^(1/1.6)
  censor.time <- pmin(rexp(n,.035),10)
  data.frame(id=seq_len(n),z=z,age=age,age10=age10,
    time=pmin(event.time,censor.time),status=as.integer(event.time<=censor.time),
    event.time=event.time,censor.time=censor.time)
}

8.6 What exactly is a tie?

Ties can arise from rounding a continuous clock, genuinely discrete event opportunities, or grouping an interval observation into one label. These mechanisms need not imply the same likelihood. In particular, an interval-censored event is not automatically an exact event at its right endpoint; Chapter 5 derived its interval probability. Here we discuss recorded exact-time analyses and state their working convention.

At a recorded event time tjt_j, let 𝒟j\mathcal D_j be the event set, dj=|𝒟j|d_j=|\mathcal D_j|, and ℛj\mathcal R_j the risk set before events. A same-time censor belongs to this denominator under the event-first convention. In the following tie formulas, SjS_j and DjD_j are sums of relative-hazard weights, not survival probabilities or event counts. Define wi=exp(βTzi),Sj=∑i∈ℛjwi,Dj=∑i∈𝒟jwi.w_i=\exp(\beta^Tz_i),\qquad S_j=\sum_{i\in\mathcal R_j}w_i,\qquad D_j=\sum_{i\in\mathcal D_j}w_i. Using the distinct-event formula djd_j times without updating anything is the Breslow approximation: LB,j(β)=∏i∈𝒟jwiSjdj.L_{B,j}(\beta)=\frac{\prod_{i\in\mathcal D_j}w_i}{S_j^{d_j}}. \label{s10-breslow}(8.8) It treats every recorded tied event as seeing the full initial risk weight. For distinct events dj=1d_j=1, this is exactly the earlier factor. For a sizable tied group, the approximation overlooks the removal of earlier failures whose latent order was not recorded.

8.7 Efron’s fractional removal

If tied failures had an unobserved order, the remaining risk weight after each failure would decrease by the weight of that particular failure. Efron’s approximation replaces those unknown removals by equal fractions of the total tied-event weight: LE,j(β)=∏i∈𝒟jwi∏ℓ=0dj−1{Sj−(ℓ/dj)Dj}.L_{E,j}(\beta)= \frac{\prod_{i\in\mathcal D_j}w_i} {\prod_{\ell=0}^{d_j-1}\{S_j-(\ell/d_j)D_j\}}. \label{s10-efron}(8.9) At the first failure nothing has been removed; before the second, 1/dj1/d_j of the tied weight is removed; and so on. This reduces to Breslow when dj=1d_j=1. It approximates sequential removal of failed risk weight without enumerating the missing order. The approximation does not identify the recording mechanism, and there is no claim here that it is uniformly best for every set of weights or every origin of ties.

The complete likelihood function sums this contribution over recorded event times. Calculating the Breslow denominator SdS^d alongside it lets us compare each fitted coefficient with the likelihood that produced it.

8.8 Two meanings of “exact”

For genuinely discrete event opportunities, suppose event odds in a risk set satisfy qi(tj)1−qi(tj)=ajexp(βTzi).\frac{q_i(t_j)}{1-q_i(t_j)}=a_j\exp(\beta^Tz_i). Conditional on exactly djd_j events, the nuisance odds multiplier cancels. The conditional probability of the observed subset is Ldisc,j(β)=∏i∈𝒟jwi∑A⊆ℛj|A|=dj∏i∈Awi.L_{\mathrm{disc},j}(\beta)= \frac{\prod_{i\in\mathcal D_j}w_i} {\displaystyle\sum_{\substack{A\subseteq\mathcal R_j\\|A|=d_j}} \prod_{i\in A}w_i}. \label{s10-discrete}(8.10) The denominator sums over subsets, not repeated selections with replacement. It is a conditional logistic likelihood. A continuous proportional-hazards model over a long recording interval does not generally imply proportional event odds; qi=1−exp(−ajwi)q_i=1-\exp(-a_jw_i) has a different form. Calling (8.10) exact names its conditional model, not every possible continuous observation process.

Directly listing all (md)\binom{m}{d} subsets is useful for a small check but quickly becomes impractical. The denominator is the degree-dd elementary symmetric sum of the mm risk weights. If Ek(m)E_k^{(m)} denotes the corresponding sum among the first mm weights, partitioning subsets according to whether they contain the last participant gives Ek(m)=Ek(m−1)+wmEk−1(m−1),E0(m)=1,Ek(0)=0(k>0).E_k^{(m)}=E_k^{(m-1)}+w_mE_{k-1}^{(m-1)},\qquad E_0^{(m)}=1,\quad E_k^{(0)}=0\ (k>0). An implementation must update degrees downward so that the same weight is not reused in the current pass. For the worked eight-person risk set:

  for (k in d:1) E[k+1] <- E[k+1]+wi*E[k]

The result is 62, matching explicit pair enumeration. The recursion computes the same conditional denominator; it does not turn a continuous observation model into a discrete one.

A different continuous-order construction averages the distinct-event partial-likelihood factors over the dj!d_j! latent orderings of the tied failures: Lord,j(β)=1dj!∑π∏ℓ=1djwπℓSj−∑r<ℓwπr.L_{\mathrm{ord},j}(\beta)=\frac1{d_j!} \sum_{\pi} \prod_{\ell=1}^{d_j} \frac{w_{\pi_\ell}}{S_j-\sum_{r<\ell}w_{\pi_r}}. \label{s10-order}(8.11) Using a sum instead of an average changes a parameter-independent factor, so maximization is unchanged. It is not the subset denominator in (8.10). The installed survival implementation’s ties="exact" uses the conditional subset likelihood, which the complete R example independently enumerates on a small cohort. Software labels must be connected to formulas rather than assumed to mean the same thing across programs.

The order construction retains the initial risk set and supposes only the tied failures are removed between these latent choices. A full likelihood for rounded times would instead integrate the event and observation model over the recording intervals, potentially including unobserved censoring order. Equation (8.11) is a partial-likelihood tie construction, not that full coarsened-data likelihood.

An analysis report should name its recording resolution, event/censor ordering, stratification variables, shared slopes, and tie method. Show a sensitivity comparison if ties are common enough for the method to matter. Avoid comparing raw likelihood values across different conditional models as if a larger value automatically selected a superior model. Distinguish numerical agreement, model adequacy, and scientific interpretation. The next chapter asks how to diagnose the remaining restrictions rather than equating successful optimization with a defensible analysis.
